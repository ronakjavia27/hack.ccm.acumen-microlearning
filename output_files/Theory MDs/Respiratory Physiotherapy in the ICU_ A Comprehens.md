

# Respiratory Physiotherapy in the ICU: A Comprehensive Note

Respiratory physiotherapy is a fundamental pillar of ICU care, targeting secretion clearance, lung volume recruitment, oxygenation optimization, and inspiratory muscle preservation in both intubated and spontaneously breathing critically ill patients. This note integrates the pathophysiological rationale, objectives, interventions, evidence base, and clinical nuances essential for bedside practice.[^1_1][^1_2]

***

## 1. Effects of Critical Illness and Intubation on the Respiratory System

Critically ill ICU patients are frequently confined to bed, sedated, and often mechanically ventilated—each factor independently contributing to multisystem deconditioning with profound effects on the respiratory system.[^1_3][^1_1]

### 1.1 Effects of Bed Rest and Immobility

Prolonged bed rest produces rapid and measurable deterioration across multiple respiratory parameters:[^1_4][^1_1]

- Reduced lung volumes (FVC, TLC) and expiratory airflow rates
- Decreased respiratory muscle strength (maximal inspiratory pressure)
- Reduced compliance of lungs and chest wall
- Impaired gas exchange and ventilation–perfusion mismatch
- Increased work of breathing (WOB)
- Impaired cough mechanism due to reduced tidal volume and respiratory muscle weakness, increasing risk of pneumonia and atelectasis
- Patients can lose 1–1.5% of total body mass per day; up to 50% in 2 weeks of strict bed rest[^1_3]
- Mechanical ventilation beyond 1 week is an independent risk factor for ICU-acquired muscle weakness[^1_3]


### 1.2 Effects of Intubation on the Respiratory System

Endotracheal intubation disrupts several innate airway defense mechanisms:[^1_5][^1_6]

- **Bypass of upper airway functions:** humidification, warming, and filtration of inspired gas are lost, leading to drying of mucosa and impaired mucociliary transport
- **Suppression of cough reflex:** the endotracheal tube prevents glottic closure, eliminating the effective cough mechanism
- **Mucociliary clearance impairment:** ciliary beat frequency is reduced by dry gases, high inspiratory flows, and altered airway geometry; secretions become retained and inspissated
- **Flow bias and secretion movement:** ventilator settings can generate an inspiratory or expiratory flow bias. When peak inspiratory flow (PIF) exceeds peak expiratory flow (PEF), secretions are pushed caudally (deeper into lungs). A PEF–PIF difference >33 L/min is considered clinically relevant for facilitating cephalad (toward mouth) secretion clearance. Commonly used ventilator settings often generate an inspiratory flow bias that may promote secretion retention[^1_6][^1_7]
- **Atelectasis:** reduced functional residual capacity (FRC), supine positioning, and absence of periodic sighs promote alveolar collapse, particularly in dependent lung regions
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP):** occurs in 9–27% of ventilated patients, with mortality of 33–55%; microaspiration of colonized oropharyngeal secretions around the ET tube cuff is a key mechanism[^1_3]


### 1.3 Ventilator-Induced Diaphragmatic Dysfunction (VIDD) and ICU-Acquired Weakness

Mechanical ventilation itself induces progressive dysfunction of the diaphragm—a condition termed **ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction (VIDD)**, which is distinct from (but often overlapping with) peripheral **ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW)**.[^1_8][^1_9]

**Pathophysiology of VIDD:**


| Mechanism | Details |
| :-- | :-- |
| Disuse atrophy | Diaphragm fiber cross-sectional area (CSA) decreases ~25% within days of controlled MV; Levine et al. showed 53–57% reduction in fast- and slow-twitch fiber CSA in brain-dead organ donors ventilated 18–69 hours [^1_10] |
| Proteolytic pathway activation | Upregulation of ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (MAFBx, MuRF-1), lysosomal-autophagy pathway activation [^1_10] |
| Oxidative stress | Increased reactive oxygen species; posttranslational protein modifications (oxidation, nitrosylation) [^1_10] |
| Ryanodine receptor dysfunction | Modified calcium release channels → enhanced calcium leak → impaired excitation–contraction coupling [^1_10] |
| Inflammation | Increased neutrophils and macrophages in diaphragm biopsies of ventilated ICU patients; sepsis plasma induces atrophy in cultured myotubes (IL-6 implicated) [^1_10] |

**Key clinical findings on diaphragm function:**

- **Demoule et al.**: 64% of 85 critically ill patients had diaphragm dysfunction (Ptr,magn <11 cmH₂O) within 24 hours of MV initiation[^1_10]
- 80% of patients ventilated >5 days develop respiratory muscle weakness at some point during their ICU stay[^1_10]
- Diaphragm dysfunction is independently associated with **sepsis** and **prolonged ventilator weaning**[^1_10]
- **Goligher et al.**: Changes in diaphragm thickness (measured by ultrasound) occurred in 56% of ventilated patients; loss of thickness correlated with low contractile activity (i.e., over-assist by ventilator)[^1_10]

**Risk factors for VIDD and ICU-AW:**

- Sepsis and systemic inflammation
- Prolonged controlled mechanical ventilation (disuse)
- Corticosteroids and prolonged neuromuscular blockade
- Hyperglycemia
- Elderly age[^1_8][^1_10]

## 2. Objectives of Respiratory Physiotherapy

The overarching aims of respiratory physiotherapy in the ICU are to maintain lung volume, improve oxygenation and ventilation, clear airway secretions, reduce the work of breathing, and enhance inspiratory muscle function—thereby preventing respiratory complications in both intubated and spontaneously breathing patients.[^1_11][^1_1]

Additional objectives include maximizing musculoskeletal function, reducing duration of mechanical ventilation, facilitating return of independent function upon ICU discharge, improving long-term quality of life, and reducing ICU and hospital length of stay.[^1_1]

### 2.1 Four Key Target Categories

| Target Category | Specific Goals |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Airway secretion clearance** | Mobilize and remove retained secretions; restore mucociliary transport; prevent mucus plugging and atelectasis [^1_1][^1_12] |
| **Lung volume recruitment / atelectasis improvement** | Re-expand collapsed alveoli; augment lung expansion; improve static lung compliance [^1_1][^1_11] |
| **Oxygenation / gas exchange** | Improve V/Q matching; enhance alveolar-capillary gas exchange; reduce shunt fraction [^1_1] |
| **Inspiratory muscle strength** | Prevent or reverse VIDD and ICU-AW; preserve cough effectiveness; facilitate ventilator weaning [^1_1][^1_10] |

## 3. Interventions and Techniques of Respiratory Physiotherapy

Physiotherapy modalities are best classified by their primary therapeutic target. Many techniques serve overlapping purposes.[^1_2][^1_1]

### 3.1 Airway Clearance Techniques

These manual or mechanical processes facilitate mobilization and removal of secretions (mucociliary clearance) from the airway. The mucociliary clearance system is normally efficient, but excess sputum production and impaired clearance mechanisms following respiratory disease, anaesthesia, and surgery necessitate intervention.[^1_1]


| Technique | Description | Key Considerations |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Manual hyperinflation (MHI)** | Self-inflating circuit delivers volume ~50% greater than tidal volume via ET/tracheostomy tube [^1_12] | Reverses acute lobar atelectasis; improves PaO₂ and static compliance; mobilizes secretions; risks: volutrauma, barotrauma, PEEP loss, ↑ICP, hemodynamic instability [^1_12] |
| **Ventilator hyperinflation (VHI)** | Uses ventilator to deliver increased tidal volume/sigh breaths | Preferred when maintaining PEEP is critical; allows continuous monitoring of airway pressures and volumes [^1_13] |
| **Positioning / postural drainage** | Gravity-assisted positioning to drain specific lung segments | Affected lobe uppermost for drainage; also improves V/Q matching; side-lying with affected side uppermost recruits collapsed alveoli [^1_1] |
| **Manual chest techniques** | Percussion (cupping/clapping), vibration, shaking applied to chest wall | Loosens secretions from bronchial walls; vibration during expiration aids cephalad mucus movement [^1_1][^1_14] |
| **Airway suctioning** (open/closed) | Catheter-based removal of secretions from ET tube or trachea | Closed-system preferred for maintaining PEEP and reducing contamination; essential after hyperinflation or manual techniques [^1_1][^1_12] |
| **Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MI:E)** | Device delivers positive pressure inspiration then rapid negative pressure expiration to simulate cough | Useful for neuromuscular patients with weak cough; can be used via ET tube or mask [^1_13] |
| **Intrapulmonary percussive ventilation (IPPV)** | Delivers mini-bursts of high-frequency gas pulsations within airway | Combines percussion, vibration, and aerosol delivery; loosens and mobilizes secretions [^1_13] |
| **Positive expiratory pressure (PEP) devices** | Flutter, Acapella—create oscillating positive pressure during expiration | For spontaneously breathing patients; splints airways during expiration, prevents airway collapse, mobilizes secretions [^1_1] |
| **Active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT)** | Cyclic sequence: breathing control → thoracic expansion exercises → forced expiration (huffing) | For cooperative spontaneously breathing patients; mobilizes and clears secretions; can be combined with positioning [^1_12] |
| **Assisted cough techniques** | Manual thoracoabdominal compression during cough effort | For patients with weakened but present cough; supplements insufficient expiratory force [^1_1] |
| **Nebulization** | Aerosolized saline, mucolytics (e.g., N-acetylcysteine), bronchodilators | Hydrates and fluidifies secretions; bronchodilators reduce airway resistance and improve secretion drainage [^1_5] |

### 3.2 Modalities for Lung Volume Recruitment / Atelectasis Improvement

| Technique | Mechanism |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Positioning** (side-lying, affected side uppermost) | Gravity-dependent alveolar recruitment; improves perfusion to ventilated regions [^1_1] |
| **Manual and ventilator hyperinflation** | Increases transpulmonary pressure; recruits collapsed alveoli via collateral ventilation channels [^1_1][^1_12] |
| **Deep breathing exercises** (segmental, resisted) | Increases tidal volume; promotes alveolar expansion in targeted lung segments [^1_1] |
| **Incentive spirometry (IS)** | Visual feedback device encourages sustained maximal inspiration; prevents post-operative atelectasis [^1_1] |
| **Breath stacking** | Patient (or ventilator) delivers consecutive inspirations without full expiration, increasing inspiratory capacity |
| **Thoracic expansion exercises (TEE)** | Deep inspiration with emphasis on expanding specific chest wall segments |
| **Rib springing** | Manual springing of ribs during expiration to facilitate inspiration recoil |
| **Manual chest wall shaking/vibrations** | Applied during expiration to augment expiratory flow and volume |
| **IPPB / NIV / CPAP** | Positive pressure delivers sustained alveolar recruitment; CPAP maintains FRC throughout respiratory cycle |

### 3.3 Modalities for Oxygenation (Gas Exchange) Improvement

| Technique | Mechanism |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Positioning** | Prone positioning dramatically improves V/Q matching in ARDS by redistributing perfusion to dorsal lung regions; upright positioning reduces abdominal pressure on diaphragm [^1_1] |
| **NIV (CPAP, BiPAP, VPAP, IPPB)** | Positive pressure recruits alveoli, reduces shunt, decreases WOB; CPAP maintains alveolar patency [^1_1] |
| **Manual or ventilator hyperinflation** | Reverses atelectasis; improves PaO₂ through alveolar recruitment [^1_1][^1_12] |
| **Breath stacking / deep breathing exercises / TEE** | Increase alveolar ventilation and recruit under-ventilated lung units [^1_1] |

### 3.4 Modalities to Maintain or Improve Inspiratory Muscle Strength

| Technique | Details |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Breathing exercises** | Diaphragmatic breathing; pursed-lip breathing; segmental expansion [^1_1] |
| **Inspiratory muscle training (IMT) devices** | Spring-loaded Threshold® IMT or POWERbreathe® device; provides resistive load during inspiration [^1_1] |
| **Incentive spirometry** | Sustained maximal inspiration with visual feedback [^1_1] |
| **Manual chest wall resistance** | Therapist applies resistance to chest wall during inspiration to load respiratory muscles [^1_1] |

**Evidence on IMT in ICU**: The largest trial randomized 69 long-term weaning facility patients to IMT or sham training; IMT significantly improved inspiratory muscle strength and facilitated weaning, with no adverse effects. A post-extubation trial (Bissett et al., n=70) showed IMT improved respiratory muscle strength (17% vs 6%, p=0.02) but did not reduce LOS or readmission risk.[^1_10]

### 3.5 Mobilisation and Rehabilitation

Early mobilization is now a key part of international guidelines and recommended bundles of care for critically ill patients. Physiotherapists play a central role in:[^1_13]

- Progressive mobility: bed exercises → sitting edge of bed → standing → marching → transfer to chair → ambulation
- Continuous passive motion (CPM) for paralyzed patients
- Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) for patients unable to actively exercise[^1_4]
- Cycle ergometry (passive or active, in-bed or over-bed)[^1_2]

**Caveat**: A large international rehabilitation trial demonstrated that higher levels of early active mobilization were not associated with benefit but were associated with an increase in adverse events, highlighting the need for judicious patient selection and progressive protocols.[^1_13]

## 4. Patient Selection, Monitoring, and Safety

### 4.1 Patient Assessment

Physiotherapists perform multisystem assessment (respiratory, cardiovascular, neurological, musculoskeletal) to formulate individualized treatment plans. Continuous evaluation is essential as the patient's physiological, motivational, and emotional condition changes frequently, necessitating a flexible care plan.[^1_15][^1_12][^1_1]

Assessment components include:

- Chest X-ray / imaging for atelectasis, consolidation, effusions
- Auscultation (added sounds, air entry)
- Secretion characteristics (volume, viscosity, color)
- Ventilator parameters (mode, tidal volume, PEEP, FiO₂, plateau pressure)
- Blood gases (PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, oxygenation index)
- Hemodynamic stability (MAP, vasopressor requirement, HR rhythm)
- Sedation level (RASS score)
- Respiratory muscle function (diaphragm ultrasound, maximal inspiratory pressure)


### 4.2 Monitoring During Treatment

- Continuous SpO₂ and ECG monitoring throughout therapy
- Ventilator waveform observation (tidal volume, airway pressures, flow–volume loops)
- Hemodynamic parameters (BP, HR) before, during, and after intervention
- Suction apparatus and emergency airway equipment immediately available
- Adrenergic bronchodilators available in case of bronchospasm[^1_14]

## 5. Contraindications and Precautions

### 5.1 Absolute Contraindications

| Contraindication | Rationale |
| :-- | :-- |
| Undrained pneumothorax | Positive pressure may worsen pneumothorax; risk of tension pneumothorax [^1_12][^1_14] |
| Unexplained hemoptysis / active pulmonary hemorrhage | Manipulation may exacerbate bleeding [^1_12][^1_14] |
| Increased intracranial pressure (ICP) | Hyperinflation and manual techniques can further raise ICP [^1_14] |
| Unstable head or neck injury | Risk of spinal cord damage with movement [^1_14] |
| Recent spinal injury | Instability risk [^1_14] |
| Rib fracture / flail chest | Percussion and vibration may worsen injury [^1_14] |
| Bronchopleural fistula | Positive pressure may worsen air leak [^1_14] |
| Acute myocardial infarction (acute phase) | Increased cardiac demand and stress [^1_5] |
| Acute pulmonary embolism | Hemodynamic instability risk [^1_5] |
| Acute pulmonary edema | May worsen with fluid redistribution [^1_5] |
| Uncontrolled hypertension | Risk of hypertensive crisis with stress [^1_14] |

### 5.2 Relative Contraindications and Precautions

- Anticoagulation therapy (risk of bleeding with percussion)
- Recent thoracic surgery (use modified gentle techniques)
- Osteoporosis / fragile ribs (use gentle or non-manual techniques)
- Severe coagulopathy
- Hemodynamic instability requiring high-dose vasopressors (individualize)[^1_14]


### 5.3 General Precautions

- Never perform chest physiotherapy immediately after meals or enteral feeds (aspiration risk)[^1_14]
- Ensure adequate analgesia and sedation as appropriate
- Always have suction and emergency airway equipment ready
- Monitor the patient continuously throughout therapy
- Modify technique intensity based on real-time patient response[^1_14]

## 6. Evidence Base

### 6.1 Systematic Review Evidence (Kayambu et al., 2013)

A systematic review of 10 RCTs found:[^1_12]

- No evidence for a mortality benefit for physiotherapy in ICU
- Improved quality of life, physical function, peripheral and respiratory muscle strength
- Increased ventilator-free days
- Decreased hospital and ICU length of stay


### 6.2 Pneumonia-Specific Evidence (van der Lee et al., 2021)

A systematic review of 8 studies in invasively ventilated adults with pneumonia found:[^1_16]

- Interventions that increased inspiratory volume benefited secretion clearance (~2 grams more, MD 1.97; 95% CI: 0.80–3.14; very low GRADE evidence)
- Increased static lung compliance immediately after treatment (MD 5.40 mL/cmH₂O)
- No adverse events
- No evidence of effect on mortality, length of stay, duration of ventilation, functional recovery, or quality of life
- **Conclusion**: Respiratory physiotherapy that increases tidal volume may benefit secretion clearance and lung compliance, but impact on patient-centred outcomes remains unclear


### 6.3 Evidence Gaps

- Lack of high-quality trials; small sample sizes in existing studies
- Optimal timing, nature, intensity ("dose"), and frequency of physiotherapy interventions remain unproven[^1_12][^1_16]
- No standard of practice exists regarding type, duration, and frequency of intervention[^1_16]
- Staff capacity impacts treatment selection, duration, and frequency[^1_13]

## 7. Clinical Pearls

1. **Intubation immediately impairs airway defenses**: The ET tube bypasses humidification, filtration, and the cough reflex within hours—anticipate secretion retention and plan proactive airway clearance.[^1_5][^1_6]
2. **Flow bias matters**: Check your ventilator's peak inspiratory vs. peak expiratory flow. An inspiratory flow bias (PIF > PEF) pushes secretions deeper into the lungs. Aim for an expiratory flow bias (PEF–PIF >33 L/min) to promote cephalad secretion movement.[^1_7][^1_6]
3. **Diaphragm dysfunction is early and common**: Up to 64% of patients have diaphragm dysfunction within 24 hours of intubation. Use diaphragm ultrasound (thickening fraction) at the bedside to monitor. Transition to partially assisted modes (e.g., pressure support) as soon as feasible to limit disuse atrophy.[^1_10]
4. **Over-assist is harmful**: Even in pressure support mode, the diaphragm may be completely inactive (detectable by absent EAdi). Excessive ventilator support causes the same disuse atrophy as controlled ventilation. Monitor and titrate support to maintain some diaphragm activity.[^1_10]
5. **Sepsis independently causes respiratory muscle weakness**: Sepsis plasma contains catabolic mediators (IL-6) that induce myotube atrophy. Treat the underlying sepsis aggressively—no specific pharmacotherapy exists to protect respiratory muscles.[^1_10]
6. **Combine techniques for synergy**: Manual or ventilator hyperinflation is most effective when combined with appropriate positioning (affected side uppermost) and manual chest wall techniques (percussion/vibration) followed by suctioning.[^1_12][^1_16]
7. **Positioning is an active intervention, not just comfort**: Prone positioning in ARDS, upright positioning to reduce diaphragmatic compression, and side-lying with affected lung uppermost for alveolar recruitment are all therapeutic—not merely for patient comfort.[^1_1]
8. **Early mobilization requires judicious selection**: While early mobilization is guideline-recommended, higher-intensity mobilization was not beneficial and increased adverse events in a large international trial. Progress patients gradually and individualize protocols.[^1_13]
9. **Inspiratory muscle training works but timing matters**: IMT improves inspiratory muscle strength and may facilitate weaning in long-term ventilated patients. However, post-extubation IMT improved strength without reducing LOS or readmissions. Optimal timing (early vs. late) and protocol remain to be determined.[^1_10]
10. **No approved pharmacotherapy for respiratory muscle weakness**: Anabolic steroids, growth hormone (which increased mortality in ICU patients), and levosimendan have been investigated—none are approved for respiratory muscle enhancement. Do not use growth hormone routinely in ICU patients.[^1_10]

## 8. Contraindication Quick Reference

| Scenario | Safe Interventions | Avoid |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Unstable hemodynamics (high-dose vasopressors) | Gentle positioning, closed suction | Manual hyperinflation, aggressive percussion, mobilization |
| Raised ICP | Gentle suction only | Hyperinflation, vibration, percussion, postural drainage head-down |
| Rib fractures / flail chest | Positioning, nebulization, deep breathing | Percussion, vibration, shaking over fracture site |
| Coagulopathy / anticoagulation | Positioning, ACBT, incentive spirometry | Percussion, vibration |
| Bronchopleural fistula | Gentle positioning, secretion management via suction | Positive pressure hyperinflation, IPPB |
| Recent thoracic surgery | Modified gentle techniques, breathing exercises | Direct percussion over surgical site |


***

The evidence base for respiratory physiotherapy in the ICU, while growing, remains limited by small studies and heterogeneous interventions. The physiological rationale for individual techniques is strong, and short-term physiological benefits (secretion clearance, lung compliance, oxygenation) are consistently demonstrated, but translation to patient-centred outcomes (mortality, LOS, quality of life) requires further high-quality research.[^1_16][^1_12]
