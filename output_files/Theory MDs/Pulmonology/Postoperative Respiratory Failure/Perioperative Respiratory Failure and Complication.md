

## Perioperative Respiratory Failure and Complications

Perioperative respiratory failure is the most serious postoperative pulmonary complication (PPC), defined by impaired gas exchange requiring escalated respiratory support after surgery. It carries a 30-day mortality of ~25% after major procedures (vs ~1% in unaffected patients) and significantly prolongs ICU/hospital stay. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

***

## Definition and Classification

### Postoperative Respiratory Failure (PRF)

Common consensus definitions include: [^1_4][^1_5][^1_6]

- Need for mechanical ventilation >48 hours postoperatively, OR
- Reintubation or reinstitution of non-invasive/invasive ventilation after extubation, OR
- Hypoxemia: PaO₂ <8 kPa (60 mmHg) on room air, PaO₂:FiO₂ <40 kPa (300 mmHg), or SpO₂ <90% requiring supplemental oxygen
- Type 2 (hypercapnic) failure: PaCO₂ >8 kPa (60 mmHg) superimposed on Type 1


### Postoperative Pulmonary Complications (PPCs) – Composite

The ARISCAT/PROVIA taskforce defined PPCs as: [^1_3][^1_7]

- Respiratory infection (pneumonia)
- Respiratory failure (as above)
- Pleural effusion
- Atelectasis
- Pneumothorax
- Bronchospasm
- Aspiration pneumonitis
- Individual severe outcomes: ARDS, pulmonary embolism

***

## Epidemiology and Impact

- **Incidence**: Up to 10% after general surgery; 22–25% after thoracic surgery for atelectasis/pneumonia/ventilation >24h; 3–10% require ventilation >48h. [^1_8][^1_9]
- **Mortality**: 14–30% of patients with a PPC die within 30 days of major surgery vs 0.2–3% without PPC. One in five patients with PRF die within 30 days. [^1_3][^1_10][^1_1]
- **Resource use**: PRF increases ICU admissions, hospital length of stay (up to +2 weeks), readmissions, and costs. [^1_2][^1_8][^1_11]

***

## Pathophysiology

Perioperative respiratory failure results from the interplay of: [^1_11][^1_5][^1_12]

- **Patient factors**: Reduced respiratory reserve (COPD, OSA, obesity, heart failure, smoking, age, frailty, poor functional status)
- **Surgical factors**: Site (upper abdominal/thoracic > lower abdominal/extremity), duration, emergency status, blood loss/transfusion
- **Anesthetic/ventilatory factors**: Atelectasis from high FiO₂, low FRC, impaired mucociliary clearance; residual neuromuscular blockade; opioid/sedative-induced hypoventilation
- **Postoperative factors**: Pain → splinting → low tidal volumes; immobility; aspiration; fluid overload → pulmonary edema

**Atelectasis** is the earliest and most frequent PPC (up to 90% under general anesthesia), promoting hypoxemia and secondary infection. [^1_5][^1_12]

***

## Risk Stratification

### Preoperative Risk Scores

- **ARISCAT score**: Widely validated PPC risk model (age, SpO₂, respiratory infection last month, preop anemia, surgical site, duration, emergency). Higher scores → markedly increased PPC risk. [^1_3]
- **Other predictors**: Low preoperative SpO₂, recent respiratory infection, anemia, upper abdominal/thoracic surgery, prolonged procedures, emergency surgery. [^1_13][^1_6]


### Intraoperative/Postoperative Risk

- High FiO₂ without PEEP, low tidal volumes without recruitment, residual neuromuscular blockade, inadequate analgesia, large fluid balances, and early postoperative hypoxemia/hypercapnia increase PRF risk. [^1_14][^1_9][^1_11]

***

## Clinical Features and Diagnosis

### Typical Presentations

- Hypoxemia (SpO₂ <90% on room air or increasing O₂ requirement)
- Tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, paradoxical breathing
- Hypercapnia (somnolence, headache, acidosis)
- New/worsening infiltrates on chest imaging
- Fever, purulent sputum (if infectious)
- Wheeze (bronchospasm), crackles (atelectasis/pneumonia/edema)


### Diagnostic Approach

- **ABG**: Confirm hypoxemia/hypercapnia; calculate PaO₂:FiO₂
- **Chest X-ray/CT**: Atelectasis, pneumonia, effusion, pneumothorax, edema
- **Labs**: CBC (leukocytosis), cultures if infection suspected
- **Bedside ultrasound**: Consolidation, effusion, pneumothorax, diaphragm function
- **Exclude mimics**: Pulmonary embolism, myocardial ischemia, transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), anaphylaxis

***

## Differential Diagnosis of Perioperative Respiratory Failure

| Category | Examples | Key Clues |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Atelectasis** | Lobar/subsegmental collapse | Early postop hypoxemia, basal crackles, CXR volume loss |
| **Pneumonia** | Hospital-acquired, aspiration | Fever, purulent sputum, new infiltrate, leukocytosis |
| **Aspiration pneumonitis/pneumonia** | Gastric content aspiration | Witnessed aspiration, chemical injury → secondary infection |
| **Pulmonary edema** | Cardiogenic, fluid overload, TRALI/TACO | Bilateral infiltrates, elevated JVP/BNP (cardiogenic), timing with transfusion |
| **ARDS** | Sepsis, trauma, pancreatitis, transfusion | PaO₂:FiO₂ ≤300 with bilateral opacities, non-cardiogenic |
| **Bronchospasm** | Asthma/COPD exacerbation, reactive airways | Expiratory wheeze, increased peak pressures |
| **Pneumothorax** | Barotrauma, line placement, surgical | Sudden hypoxemia, unilateral absent breath sounds, CXR/US |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Postoperative hypercoagulability | Sudden hypoxemia, tachycardia, pleuritic pain, CTA |
| **Residual neuromuscular blockade** | Incomplete reversal | Hypoventilation, weak cough, elevated CO₂ |
| **Mechanical complications** | Bronchopleural fistula, lung torsion (thoracic) | Post-thoracic surgery, air leak, persistent collapse |

[^1_5][^1_9][^1_7][^1_12]

## Prevention Strategies

### Preoperative Optimization

- Smoking cessation (≥4 weeks ideal), treat active respiratory infections, optimize COPD/asthma/OSA (continue inhalers, CPAP), correct anemia, encourage prehabilitation. [^1_2][^1_13]
- Risk stratify using ARISCAT; plan postoperative monitoring level accordingly. [^1_3]


### Intraoperative Measures

- **Lung-protective ventilation**: Low tidal volume (6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight), moderate PEEP, recruitment maneuvers, avoid excessive FiO₂ (target SpO₂ 92–96%). [^1_5][^1_14][^1_13]
- **Pressure-controlled ventilation** may reduce PPCs vs volume-controlled in some studies. [^1_14]
- Minimize neuromuscular blocker dose; ensure complete reversal (quantitative monitoring). [^1_9][^1_11]
- Goal-directed fluid therapy to avoid overload. [^1_13]


### Postoperative Measures

- Adequate analgesia (multimodal, regional techniques where feasible) to enable deep breathing/coughing. [^1_5][^1_13]
- Early mobilization, incentive spirometry, chest physiotherapy. [^1_5][^1_4]
- **Respiratory support in high-risk patients**: HFNO, CPAP, or NIV reduces PPCs and reintubation after abdominal/thoracic/cardiac surgery, especially in OSA/COPD. [^1_5][^1_14]
- Continue home CPAP for OSA patients; monitor closely in PACU/ward. [^1_5][^1_11]

***

## Management of Established Respiratory Failure

### Initial Stabilization

- **ABCs**, supplemental oxygen to target SpO₂ 92–96% (avoid hyperoxia).
- Optimize analgesia (avoid oversedation), treat bronchospasm (bronchodilators, steroids if indicated), encourage lung expansion (physiotherapy, positioning). [^1_5]


### Escalation of Respiratory Support

- **HFNO/CPAP/NIV**: First-line for mild–moderate failure without contraindications; superior to conventional O₂ in high-risk postoperative patients. [^1_5]
- **Invasive mechanical ventilation** if:
    - Severe hypoxemia/hypercapnia with acidosis
    - Respiratory muscle fatigue (tachypnea, accessory muscle use)
    - Altered mental status, hemodynamic instability, inability to protect airway
    - Failure of NIV/HFNO


### Treat Underlying Cause

- **Atelectasis**: Aggressive physiotherapy, recruitment maneuvers, bronchoscopy if lobar collapse with mucus plug. [^1_9]
- **Pneumonia**: Appropriate antibiotics per local antibiogram, source control. [^1_5]
- **Pulmonary edema**: Diuretics, fluid restriction, treat cardiac dysfunction.
- **ARDS**: Lung-protective ventilation (4–6 mL/kg PBW), appropriate PEEP, conservative fluids, consider proning if severe. [^1_1]
- **Pneumothorax**: Needle decompression/chest drain as indicated.
- **Residual neuromuscular blockade**: Supportive ventilation until recovery; consider reversal agents if appropriate. [^1_9]
- **Mechanical complications** (e.g., bronchopleural fistula, lung torsion): Surgical/IR consultation. [^1_9][^1_7]

***

## Special Considerations

### Thoracic Surgery

- Higher PPC rates (22–25% for atelectasis/pneumonia/ventilation >24h; 3–10% for ventilation >48h). [^1_9]
- Watch for bronchopleural fistula, lung torsion, persistent air leak, pneumothorax. [^1_9][^1_7]


### Obesity/OSA

- High risk for hypoventilation, atelectasis, desaturation. Use CPAP/NIV early; avoid excessive opioids/sedatives. [^1_5][^1_11]


### Elderly/Frail

- Reduced reserve, higher PPC and mortality; prioritize lung-protective strategies and early mobilization. [^1_3][^1_6]

***

## Prognosis

- PRF increases 30-day mortality ~10-fold after abdominal surgery; overall PRF mortality ~25% after major surgery. [^1_8][^1_1]
- PPCs predict both short- and long-term mortality; one in five PPC patients die within 30 days of major surgery. [^1_3]
- Survivors have prolonged recovery, higher readmission, and discharge to skilled facilities. [^1_8][^1_11]

***

## Topic Pearls

- ★ **PRF definition**: Ventilation >48h or reintubation/NIV after extubation, or significant hypoxemia/hypercapnia post-op. [^1_4][^1_5]
- ★ **Atelectasis** is the earliest/most common PPC (up to 90%) and a key driver of hypoxemia and pneumonia. [^1_5][^1_12]
- ★ **Mortality**: ~25% with PRF after major surgery; PPCs increase 30-day mortality 5–10×. [^1_1][^1_8][^1_3]
- ★ **Prevention**: Lung-protective ventilation (low Vt, PEEP, recruitment), complete NMB reversal, adequate analgesia, early mobilization, CPAP/NIV in high-risk. [^1_14][^1_5][^1_13]
- ★ **High-risk surgery**: Upper abdominal/thoracic, prolonged, emergency procedures; use ARISCAT for risk stratification. [^1_3][^1_9]
- ★ **Management**: Escalate from O₂ → HFNO/CPAP/NIV → intubation based on severity, fatigue, and comorbidities; treat underlying cause. [^1_5]
- ★ **Red flags**: Sudden hypoxemia post-thoracic surgery → consider pneumothorax, lung torsion, bronchopleural fistula. [^1_9][^1_7]
