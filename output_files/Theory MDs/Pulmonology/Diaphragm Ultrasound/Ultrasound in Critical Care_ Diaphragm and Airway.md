
## Ultrasound in Critical Care: Diaphragm and Airway Assessment

Ultrasound has emerged as an indispensable bedside tool in the ICU for two critical applications: assessing diaphragm function to guide weaning from mechanical ventilation, and evaluating airway anatomy to optimize airway management decisions. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3] Both applications leverage the portability, non-invasiveness, and real-time imaging capability of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) to enhance clinical decision-making beyond traditional parameters. [^1_4][^1_5][^1_6]

***

## Part 1: Diaphragm Ultrasound in Weaning Readiness

### Definition and Rationale

Diaphragm ultrasound provides a **non-invasive, real-time, radiation-free** method to assess diaphragmatic structure and function at the bedside. [^1_1][^1_7][^1_8] Traditional weaning parameters like RSBI (Rapid Shallow Breathing Index) have limited specificity and fail to account for diaphragmatic dysfunction, a major cause of weaning failure. [^1_1][^1_9][^1_4] Diaphragm ultrasound directly evaluates the primary inspiratory muscle, offering superior predictive accuracy for weaning outcomes. [^1_7][^1_10][^1_11]

### Classification of Diaphragm Ultrasound Parameters

Two primary sonographic indices are used:


| Parameter | What It Measures | Clinical Significance |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Diaphragmatic Excursion (DE)** | Distance the diaphragm moves during respiration (cm) | Reflects global diaphragm function and strength; integrates neural drive and muscle contractility [^1_1][^1_8] |
| **Diaphragm Thickening Fraction (DTF)** | Percentage change in diaphragm thickness from expiration to inspiration | Analogous to cardiac ejection fraction; measures contractility of diaphragm fibers [^1_8][^1_12] |

### Pathophysiology: Why Diaphragm Dysfunction Matters

- **Ventilator-Induced Diaphragm Dysfunction (VIDD):** Prolonged mechanical ventilation leads to diaphragmatic atrophy and weakness within hours. [^1_9][^1_10]
- **Diaphragmatic dysfunction (DD)** accounts for a significant proportion of extubation failures and prolonged weaning. [^1_7][^1_9]
- Early identification of DD allows for tailored weaning strategies, respiratory muscle training, or delayed extubation in high-risk patients. [^1_13][^1_8]


### Technique: How to Perform Diaphragm Ultrasound

#### Patient Positioning

- Semi-recumbent position at **30–45°**. [^1_1]
- Ensure patient is cooperative and breathing spontaneously (during SBT).


#### Probe Selection

- **DE measurement:** Low-frequency curvilinear probe (**3–5 MHz**). [^1_1][^1_8]
- **DTF measurement:** High-frequency linear probe (**8–13 MHz**). [^1_1][^1_8]


#### Diaphragmatic Excursion (DE) Measurement

**Anatomical landmark:** Right hemidiaphragm (better hepatic acoustic window). [^1_1][^1_12]

**Steps:**

1. Place curvilinear probe in the **right subcostal margin**, angled cranially toward the diaphragm dome. [^1_1]
2. Use **B-mode** to visualize the diaphragm as a hyperechoic line.
3. Switch to **M-mode** with the cursor perpendicular to the diaphragm. [^1_1][^1_4]
4. Ask patient to perform **maximal inspiration** (from residual volume to total lung capacity).
5. Measure the **maximum vertical displacement** from baseline in cm. [^1_1][^1_8]

**Normal values:**

- **DE >1.2 cm** predicts successful weaning (sensitivity 93–97%, specificity 71–82%). [^1_1][^1_4][^1_8]
- **DE <1.0 cm** suggests diaphragmatic dysfunction and high risk of weaning failure. [^1_8][^1_2]


#### Diaphragm Thickening Fraction (DTF) Measurement

**Anatomical landmark:** Zone of apposition at the **8th–10th intercostal space**, between anterior and mid-axillary lines. [^1_1][^1_12]

**Steps:**

1. Place linear probe **perpendicular to the chest wall** at the zone of apposition. [^1_1][^1_12]
2. Visualize diaphragm as a **three-layer structure**: pleura (hyperechoic), muscle (hypoechoic), peritoneum (hyperechoic). [^1_1][^1_8]
3. Use **M-mode** to measure thickness at:
    - **End-expiration (TDmin)**
    - **End-inspiration (TDmax)**
4. Calculate DTF using formula:

$$
\text{DTF (\%)} = \frac{\text{TDmax} - \text{TDmin}}{\text{TDmin}} \times 100
$$

[^1_1][^1_4][^1_12]

**Normal values:**

- **DTF >30–36%** predicts successful weaning (sensitivity 80–91%, specificity 52–88%). [^1_1][^1_8][^1_14]
- **DTF <20–25%** indicates severe diaphragmatic dysfunction. [^1_15][^1_8]


### Diagnostic Accuracy: Evidence Summary

| Parameter | Cut-off | Sensitivity | Specificity | AUROC | Study |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **DE** | >1.21 cm | 94% | 71% | 0.81 | Saravanan et al. 2022 [^1_1] |
| **DTF** | >37% | 80% | 52% | 0.65 | Saravanan et al. 2022 [^1_1] |
| **DE** | >1.25 cm | 97% | 82% | 0.81 | Kim et al. 2018 [^1_4] |
| **DTF** | >21.5% | 91% | 61% | 0.66 | Kim et al. 2018 [^1_4] |
| **DTF** (pooled) | — | 70% | 84% | 0.82 | Meta-analysis [^1_11] |
| **DE** (pooled) | — | 71% | 80% | 0.82 | Meta-analysis [^1_11] |

**Key findings:**

- **DE is the best standalone predictor** of weaning success (AUROC 0.81). [^1_1][^1_4]
- **DTF has higher specificity** but lower sensitivity compared to DE. [^1_1][^1_11]
- **Both DE and DTF outperform RSBI** (AUROC 0.42 for RSBI vs. 0.81 for DE). [^1_1]
- Combining **RSBI + DE** or **RSBI + DTF** improves accuracy over RSBI alone. [^1_1][^1_8]


### When to Use Diaphragm Ultrasound in Weaning

**Indications:**

- Patients mechanically ventilated **>24–48 hours** undergoing SBT. [^1_1][^1_8]
- Patients with **previous weaning failure** or prolonged weaning. [^1_7][^1_10]
- High-risk patients: COPD, neuromuscular disease, prolonged sedation, sepsis. [^1_9][^1_16]
- When clinical parameters are equivocal or conflicting. [^1_13][^1_8]

**Timing:**

- Perform **during SBT** (PSV 5–8 cm H₂O or T-piece) before extubation decision. [^1_1][^1_4]
- Can be repeated serially to track diaphragm recovery over days. [^1_13][^1_17]


### Clinical Algorithm: Diaphragm Ultrasound in Weaning

```
Step 1: Patient meets clinical weaning criteria (GCS ≥10, stable hemodynamics, adequate cough)
        ↓
Step 2: Perform SBT (30–120 min)
        ↓
Step 3: Measure DE and/or DTF during SBT
        ↓
Step 4: Interpret results:
        - DE >1.2 cm AND DTF >30% → High likelihood of weaning success → Proceed to extubation
        - DE <1.0 cm OR DTF <20% → High risk of failure → Consider delayed extubation, NIV support, or respiratory muscle training
        - Borderline values (DE 1.0–1.2 cm, DTF 20–30%) → Integrate with RSBI, clinical assessment, and comorbidities
        ↓
Step 5: Post-extubation monitoring for 48 hours (reintubation/NIV requirement = failure)
```


### Limitations and Pitfalls

- **Inter-operator variability:** Requires training and standardization. [^1_10][^1_11]
- **Confounding factors:**
    - Abdominal compliance (obesity, ascites, pregnancy) affects DE. [^1_8][^1_16]
    - Rib cage and accessory muscle contribution may mask diaphragm weakness. [^1_8]
    - COPD patients may have preserved DE despite reduced DTF. [^1_8][^1_5]
- **Heterogeneity in cut-off values** across studies (DE 1.0–1.4 cm, DTF 20–36%). [^1_1][^1_17][^1_11]
- **Not a standalone test:** Must integrate with clinical judgment, RSBI, and comorbidities. [^1_1][^1_18]


### Landmark Trials and Guidelines

- **Saravanan et al. 2022 (n=200):** DE >1.21 cm best predictor (AUROC 0.81). [^1_1]
- **Kim et al. 2018 (n=200):** DE >1.25 cm (sensitivity 97%, specificity 82%). [^1_4]
- **Meta-analysis 2021 (n=816):** Pooled AUROC 0.82 for both DE and DTF. [^1_11]
- **Zambon et al. 2017 (systematic review):** Confirmed feasibility and reproducibility of diaphragm ultrasound in ICU. [^1_16]


### Clinical Pearls

- **DE is easier to measure** and more robust than DTF in obese or uncooperative patients.
- **Paradoxical diaphragm motion** (upward movement during inspiration) indicates severe dysfunction and near-certain weaning failure.
- **Serial measurements** (e.g., daily DTF) can track diaphragm recovery and guide timing of SBT.
- **Combine DE + RSBI** for optimal prediction (AUROC 0.81 vs. 0.42 for RSBI alone). [^1_1]
- **DTF <20%** in a patient with prolonged ventilation suggests VIDD and may warrant delayed weaning or diaphragm pacing trials.

## Part 2: Airway Ultrasound in Critical Care

### Definition and Scope

Airway ultrasound uses high-frequency probes to visualize **supraglottic, glottic, and subglottic structures** for pre-intubation assessment, procedural guidance, and post-extubation complication prediction. [^1_2][^1_3][^1_5] It complements (but does not replace) direct laryngoscopy and clinical airway assessment. [^1_19][^1_20]

### Clinical Applications in ICU

| Application | Clinical Question | Ultrasound Parameter |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Pre-intubation assessment** | Difficult airway? | Skin-to-epiglottis distance, hyomental distance ratio, tongue thickness [^1_6][^1_21] |
| **ETT placement confirmation** | Tracheal vs. esophageal intubation? | Direct visualization of ETT in trachea, lung sliding, diaphragm motion [^1_16][^1_22] |
| **Cricothyroid membrane localization** | Emergency surgical airway? | CTM identification and marking [^1_14][^1_23] |
| **Post-extubation stridor prediction** | Risk of laryngeal edema? | Air column width (ACW) at vocal cords pre/post cuff deflation [^1_19][^1_24] |
| **Tracheostomy guidance** | Safe percutaneous tracheostomy? | Tracheal ring counting, vascular mapping, CTM localization [^1_14][^1_25] |
| **Vocal cord assessment** | Vocal cord paralysis? | Dynamic vocal cord motion during respiration [^1_19][^1_5] |

### Technique: How to Perform Airway Ultrasound

#### Probe Selection

- **Linear high-frequency probe (10–15 MHz):** Anterior neck structures (thyroid, cricoid, vocal cords, CTM). [^1_16][^1_22]
- **Curvilinear low-frequency probe (3–5 MHz):** Deeper structures (tracheal rings, subglottic diameter in obese patients). [^1_16]


#### Patient Positioning

- **Supine with neck extended** (sniffing position) for optimal visualization. [^1_26][^1_22]
- For CTM localization, slight neck extension improves accuracy. [^1_26]


### Pre-Intubation Airway Assessment

**Predictors of Difficult Intubation:**


| Ultrasound Parameter | Cut-off | Interpretation |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Skin-to-epiglottis distance (SEDD)** | >2.75 cm | Difficult laryngoscopy (sensitivity 85%, specificity 90%) [^1_6] |
| **Hyomental distance ratio (HMDR)** | <1.2 | Difficult airway (better than Mallampati in obese patients) [^1_6][^1_21] |
| **Tongue thickness** | >5.5 cm | Associated with difficult intubation [^1_6] |
| **Anterior neck soft tissue thickness** | >2.5 cm at thyrohyoid membrane | Predicts difficult laryngoscopy [^1_5] |

**Technique for SEDD:**

1. Place linear probe **transversely** at the level of the thyrohyoid membrane.
2. Measure distance from **skin surface to anterior epiglottis** in cm. [^1_6]

**Technique for HMDR:**

1. Measure **hyomental distance** at rest (neck neutral).
2. Measure **hyomental distance** at maximal neck extension.
3. Calculate ratio: HMDR = (distance at extension) / (distance at rest). [^1_6]

### Endotracheal Tube Placement Confirmation

**Direct method (real-time during intubation):**

1. Place linear probe **transversely** at the suprasternal notch or cricothyroid membrane. [^1_16][^1_22]
2. Visualize trachea as a **hyperechoic semicircle** with posterior acoustic shadow.
3. Observe ETT passing through vocal cords into trachea (seen as **two parallel hyperechoic lines** with reverberation artifact). [^1_16][^1_20]
4. Confirm bilateral lung sliding and diaphragm excursion post-intubation. [^1_16][^1_22]

**Indirect method (post-intubation):**

- **Lung sliding:** Presence confirms tracheal intubation; absence suggests esophageal intubation or mainstem bronchial intubation. [^1_16][^1_22]
- **Diaphragm motion:** Symmetric diaphragmatic excursion confirms bilateral ventilation. [^1_22]

**Accuracy:**

- **Sensitivity 98.9%, specificity 100%** for tracheal vs. esophageal intubation. [^1_6]
- Faster than capnography in cardiac arrest (no CO₂ production). [^1_20][^1_22]


### Cricothyroid Membrane (CTM) Localization

**Indications:**

- Emergency cricothyroidotomy in "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" (CICO) scenarios. [^1_23][^1_22]
- Pre-marking CTM in anticipated difficult airways (obesity, neck masses, trauma). [^1_26][^1_23]

**Technique:**

1. Place linear probe **longitudinally** in the midline of the anterior neck.
2. Identify **thyroid cartilage** (superior, V-shaped), **cricoid cartilage** (inferior, complete ring), and **CTM** (hypoechoic gap between them). [^1_16][^1_23]
3. Mark CTM location with skin marker. [^1_26][^1_23]

**Accuracy:**

- **Palpation success:** 50–70% (lower in obese females). [^1_23]
- **Ultrasound success:** 95–100%, even in obese patients. [^1_6][^1_23]
- Reduces time to surgical airway by 30–50%. [^1_6]


### Post-Extubation Stridor Prediction

**Rationale:**

- Post-extubation stridor occurs in 5–15% of ICU patients and is a marker of laryngeal edema. [^1_19][^1_24]
- Traditional **cuff leak test** (CLV <110 mL) has limited sensitivity. [^1_24]
- Ultrasound measures **air column width (ACW)** at the vocal cords before and after cuff deflation. [^1_19][^1_24]

**Technique:**

1. Place linear probe **transversely** at the level of the vocal cords (thyroid cartilage).
2. Visualize **air column** as an anechoic (black) space between vocal cords. [^1_19][^1_24]
3. Measure **ACW before cuff deflation** (ACW-pre).
4. Deflate ETT cuff and measure **ACW after deflation** (ACW-post).
5. Calculate **ACW difference (ACWD)**: ACWD = ACW-post − ACW-pre. [^1_24]

**Interpretation:**

- **ACW-post <4.5 mm** or **ACWD <1.0 mm** predicts post-extubation stridor (sensitivity 85%, specificity 90%). [^1_19][^1_24]
- Patients with stridor: ACW-post 4.5 ± 0.8 mm vs. no stridor: 6.4 ± 2.0 mm. [^1_19][^1_24]

**Clinical algorithm:**

```
Step 1: Patient ready for extubation (passes SBT, adequate cough, GCS ≥10)
        ↓
Step 2: Perform cuff leak test (CLV >110 mL = low risk)
        ↓
Step 3: If CLV <110 mL or high-risk features (prolonged intubation, large ETT, traumatic intubation):
        - Measure ACW pre/post cuff deflation
        - ACW-post <4.5 mm OR ACWD <1.0 mm → High risk of stridor
        - Administer IV steroids (dexamethasone 0.1 mg/kg or methylprednisolone 20 mg Q4H × 3 doses) 4–6 hours before extubation [web:23]
        - Consider delayed extubation or prophylactic NIV post-extubation
        ↓
Step 4: Post-extubation monitoring for stridor (auditory + clinical assessment)
```


### Tracheostomy Guidance

**Pre-procedure assessment:**

- Identify **tracheal rings** (count from cricoid downward).
- Map **anterior neck vessels** (thyroid ima artery, jugular veins) to avoid vascular injury. [^1_14][^1_25]
- Measure **tracheal diameter** at intended puncture site. [^1_14]

**Intra-procedure guidance:**

- Real-time needle visualization during puncture.
- Confirm tracheal entry (air artifact, loss of resistance). [^1_14][^1_25]

**Benefits:**

- Reduces complications (bleeding, false passage, posterior tracheal wall injury). [^1_14][^1_25]
- Shortens procedure time in obese or anatomically distorted necks. [^1_14]


### Limitations and Pitfalls

- **Operator dependency:** Requires training in airway sonoanatomy. [^1_3][^1_6]
- **Air artifact:** Limits visualization in patients with subcutaneous emphysema or obesity. [^1_5][^1_22]
- **Dynamic assessment required:** Static images are insufficient; must observe real-time motion (vocal cords, lung sliding). [^1_22]
- **Not a replacement for clinical judgment:** Integrate with Mallampati score, thyromental distance, and direct laryngoscopy. [^1_5][^1_20]


### Evidence and Guidelines

- **Difficult Airway Society (DAS) 2025 Guidelines:** Recommend ultrasound for pre-intubation airway assessment in high-risk patients (obesity, neck masses, previous difficult airway). [^1_26][^1_21][^1_27]
- **Meta-analysis 2025:** Ultrasound parameters (SEDD, HMDR) outperform traditional clinical predictors in obese patients. [^1_6][^1_21]
- **Randomized trials:** Ultrasound-guided CTM localization improves success rates from 50% to 98%. [^1_6][^1_23]


### Clinical Pearls

- **Always extend the neck** during airway ultrasound; it improves visualization and mimics intubation position. [^1_26][^1_22]
- **Use lung sliding** as a rapid confirmatory sign of tracheal intubation (especially in cardiac arrest when capnography is unreliable). [^1_16][^1_22]
- **ACW measurement** is more sensitive than cuff leak test for predicting post-extubation stridor. [^1_24]
- **Mark the CTM** before inducing anesthesia in anticipated difficult airways—palpation fails in 30% of obese females. [^1_26][^1_23]
- **Vocal cord paralysis** appears as immobile, paramedian-positioned cords on dynamic ultrasound. [^1_19][^1_5]

## Integration into ICU Practice: A Practical Framework

### When to Order Diaphragm Ultrasound

- **Day 1–2 of ventilation:** Baseline DE/DTF in high-risk patients (sepsis, COPD, neuromuscular disease).
- **Before every SBT:** If patient has been ventilated >48 hours or has failed prior SBT.
- **After failed extubation:** Assess for VIDD before re-attempting weaning.


### When to Order Airway Ultrasound

- **Pre-intubation:** Obesity (BMI >35), neck masses, limited neck mobility, history of difficult intubation.
- **During intubation:** Real-time confirmation in cardiac arrest or when capnography is unreliable.
- **Before extubation:** Prolonged intubation (>7 days), traumatic intubation, large ETT, prior failed extubation.
- **Before cricothyroidotomy:** Mark CTM in all difficult airway carts; use ultrasound in emergency if time permits.


### Documentation Template

**Diaphragm Ultrasound Report:**

- Probe type: Curvilinear 3–5 MHz (DE) / Linear 10–13 MHz (DTF)
- Position: Semi-recumbent 45°
- DE: ___ cm (right hemidiaphragm, M-mode)
- DTF: ___% (TDmax ___ mm, TDmin ___ mm)
- Interpretation: Normal / Mild dysfunction / Severe dysfunction
- Recommendation: Proceed to extubation / Delay weaning / Consider NIV support

**Airway Ultrasound Report:**

- Probe type: Linear 10–15 MHz
- Position: Supine, neck extended
- SEDD: ___ cm
- HMDR: ___
- CTM localization: Successful / Failed (marked at ___ cm from sternal notch)
- ACW pre-cuff deflation: ___ mm
- ACW post-cuff deflation: ___ mm
- ACWD: ___ mm
- ETT placement: Tracheal / Esophageal (direct visualization + lung sliding)
- Interpretation: Low risk / High risk for difficult intubation or post-extubation stridor
- Recommendation: Proceed with intubation / Prepare for difficult airway / Administer steroids before extubation

## Key Takeaways for Examinations

1. **Diaphragm ultrasound parameters:**
    - **DE >1.2 cm** and **DTF >30–36%** predict successful weaning. [^1_1][^1_8]
    - **DE is superior to RSBI** (AUROC 0.81 vs. 0.42). [^1_1]
    - Measure during SBT using M-mode. [^1_1][^1_4]
2. **Airway ultrasound applications:**
    - **SEDD >2.75 cm** and **HMDR <1.2** predict difficult intubation. [^1_6]
    - **ACW <4.5 mm post-cuff deflation** predicts post-extubation stridor. [^1_19][^1_24]
    - **Ultrasound-guided CTM localization** improves success from 50% to 98%. [^1_6][^1_23]
3. **Probe selection:**
    - **Curvilinear 3–5 MHz:** Diaphragm excursion, deep airway structures.
    - **Linear 10–15 MHz:** Diaphragm thickening, anterior neck (vocal cords, CTM, tracheal rings).
4. **Clinical integration:**
    - Use ultrasound to **complement, not replace**, clinical assessment.
    - Combine **DE + RSBI** or **ACW + cuff leak test** for optimal prediction.
    - Perform **serial measurements** to track recovery or progression.
