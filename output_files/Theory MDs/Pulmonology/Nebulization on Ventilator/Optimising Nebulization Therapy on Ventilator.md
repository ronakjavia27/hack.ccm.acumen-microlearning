
## Optimising Nebulization Therapy on Ventilator

Optimising nebulisation on a ventilator means: using the right device, in the right place, with the right ventilator settings, so the maximum drug reaches the lungs while keeping the circuit safe and closed. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

***

## 1. Why is it tricky on a ventilator?

- The ventilator circuit is long and humidified → aerosol particles can stick to the tubing or grow in size and deposit before reaching the lungs. [^1_1][^1_4]
- Ventilator flow patterns, filters, and humidifiers all reduce the **delivered dose**. [^1_2][^1_5]
- In ICU, we also worry about infection control and maintaining a closed circuit. [^1_6]

So the goal is to **maximise lung deposition** and **minimise losses**.

***

## 2. Device choice: which nebuliser?

### a) Jet (pneumatic) nebuliser

- Uses ventilator or external gas flow (6–10 L/min). [^1_7][^1_8]
- Common, cheap, but more drug loss in the circuit. [^1_2]
- Best when integrated with ventilator’s “nebuliser mode” (synchronised with inspiration). [^1_2]


### b) Vibrating Mesh Nebuliser (VMN)

- More efficient, smaller, less residual volume. [^1_3][^1_5]
- Can be placed closer to the patient; often left in the circuit during treatment. [^1_2][^1_3]
- Preferred for expensive or critical drugs (e.g., antibiotics, prostacyclins).


### c) Ultrasonic nebuliser

- Less commonly used in ventilated adults; more for specific situations.
- Generally not first-line for bronchodilators in ICU.

**Exam tip:** If asked “best device for ventilated patient” → **Vibrating mesh** is often the answer for efficiency; **jet** is acceptable if that’s all that’s available.

***

## 3. Where to place the nebuliser in the circuit?

Correct position reduces rain-out and improves delivery.

- **Jet nebuliser:**
    - Place on the **inspiratory limb**, **15–30 cm** from the Y-piece (or ~18 inches / 45 cm from the patient). [^1_1][^1_7][^1_8][^1_3]
    - This allows aerosol to stabilise and reduces large droplets hitting the tube.
- **Mesh nebuliser:**
    - Can be placed **closer to the patient**, even within 15 cm of the Y-piece. [^1_3]
    - Some guidelines allow placement between Y-piece and ET tube if the device is designed for it.
- **Avoid** placing nebuliser after the humidifier if possible, or at least ensure it’s on the inspiratory side and not too close to the humidifier chamber. [^1_9]

**Exam memory aid:**
“**Jet = a bit away; Mesh = close to Y**”

***

## 4. Ventilator settings to optimise aerosol delivery

You want **slower, longer, bigger breaths** so aerosol has time to travel and deposit.

### Key adjustments during nebulisation:

- **Mode:** Prefer **volume-controlled ventilation (VCV)** during treatment if possible. [^1_3]
- **Tidal volume (Vt):** Temporarily increase to **8–10 mL/kg** if clinically safe (especially in obstructive disease). [^1_3]
- **Inspiratory time (Ti):** Increase to **>1 second**; aim for **Ti/Ttot ≥ 0.3–0.5**. [^1_7][^1_3][^1_10]
- **Inspiratory flow:**
    - For jet: use ventilator’s nebuliser synchronisation if available.
    - Otherwise, set external gas flow **6–10 L/min** to the nebuliser. [^1_7][^1_8]
- **Respiratory rate:** Consider **reducing RR** slightly to prolong inspiratory time (if safe). [^1_3]
- **PEEP:** Usually keep as prescribed; in stable patients, a small reduction may be considered, but not routine. [^1_3]

**Exam one-liner:**
“During nebulisation: **increase inspiratory time, increase Vt, reduce flow turbulence**.”

***

## 5. Humidification and filters

### Heat and Moisture Exchanger (HME) / Humidifier

- **HME (filter type):**
    - Remove HME from between the nebuliser and patient if possible; it can reduce inhaled dose by up to **40%**. [^1_2]
    - If using an aerosol-permeable HME, keep it but **increase nominal dose** accordingly.
- **Active heated humidifier:**
    - Ideally **turn off or bypass** during nebulisation to reduce particle growth and rain-out (“dry circuit” technique). [^1_3][^1_9]
    - If your protocol says not to disconnect, at least **do not increase humidity** during treatment and **resume full humidification immediately after**. [^1_2][^1_3]


### Expiratory filter

- Place a **low-resistance bacterial/viral filter** on the **expiratory limb** to:
    - Protect the ventilator from contamination.
    - Prevent medicated aerosol from escaping into the environment. [^1_9][^1_6]
- Check for **increased airway resistance** after therapy; a clogged expiratory filter can mimic bronchospasm. [^1_2]

**Exam point:** If a question mentions “nebulisation in ventilated patient with infection risk” → think **closed circuit + expiratory filter**.

***

## 6. Step-by-step practical approach (exam-friendly)

### Before nebulisation

1. **Assess patient:**
    - Indication (bronchospasm, secretions, specific drug).
    - Airway patency; suction if excessive secretions. [^1_8][^1_11]
2. **Choose device \& drug:**
    - Jet vs mesh based on drug, availability, and cost.
    - Check dose and dilution; use **single-patient-use nebuliser cups**. [^1_3]
3. **Prepare ventilator:**
    - Ensure circuit is closed; check for leaks.
    - Confirm ventilator has nebuliser mode or plan for external gas.

### During set-up

4. **Position the nebuliser:**
    - Jet: 15–30 cm from Y-piece on inspiratory limb.
    - Mesh: closer to Y-piece as per manufacturer. [^1_1][^1_3]
5. **Adjust humidification:**
    - Remove/bypass HME if possible.
    - Preferably turn off active humidifier temporarily (“dry circuit”). [^1_2][^1_3]
6. **Optimise ventilator settings:**
    - Mode: VCV if possible.
    - Vt: 8–10 mL/kg (if safe).
    - Ti: >1 s; Ti/Ttot ≥ 0.3–0.5.
    - Reduce RR slightly if needed. [^1_7][^1_3]
7. **Connect gas source:**
    - For jet: 6–10 L/min from ventilator or external source.
    - Ensure ventilator alarms are adjusted for added flow/volume. [^1_7][^1_8]

### During treatment

8. **Start nebulisation:**
    - Observe aerosol cloud in the chamber.
    - Ensure no leaks and that the nebuliser is functioning throughout. [^1_2][^1_11]
9. **Monitor patient:**
    - Watch for changes in HR, BP, SpO₂, wheeze, work of breathing.
    - Be ready to stop if severe bronchospasm or desaturation occurs. [^1_8][^1_11]

### After treatment

10. **Stop nebuliser when medication is spent** (no more mist, cup nearly empty). [^1_7]
11. **Remove/bypass steps:**
    - Remove open-cup nebuliser from circuit (VMN may stay if allowed).
    - Reconnect HME / turn humidifier back on immediately to prevent mucus plugging. [^1_2][^1_3]
12. **Return ventilator settings** to baseline. [^1_2][^1_8]
13. **Check expiratory filter** for blockage; replace if resistance increased. [^1_2]
14. **Document** drug, dose, device, response, and any adverse events.

***

## 7. Special points for exams

- **Fill volume:**
    - Jet nebuliser: **4–5 mL** total (drug + saline) is typical to prolong treatment and improve aerosolisation. [^1_7][^1_12][^1_9]
- **Flow to nebuliser:**
    - **6–10 L/min** for jet nebulisers. [^1_7][^1_8]
- **Distance from patient:**
    - Jet: **15–30 cm** from Y-piece on inspiratory limb. [^1_1][^1_7]
    - Mesh: can be closer, even near Y-piece. [^1_3]
- **Inspiratory time:**
    - Aim for **Ti > 1 s**, **Ti/Ttot ≥ 0.3–0.5**. [^1_7][^1_3][^1_10]
- **Humidification:**
    - Turn off or bypass if possible during treatment; resume immediately after. [^1_3][^1_9]
- **Circuit safety:**
    - Keep circuit closed; use expiratory filter; avoid breaking connections in infectious patients. [^1_8][^1_6]

***

## 8. Quick “one-page” memory checklist

**Before:**

- Indication + airway clear (suction if needed).
- Device: jet vs mesh.
- Dose and fill volume (4–5 mL for jet).

**Set-up:**

- Nebuliser on inspiratory limb, 15–30 cm from Y (jet).
- Remove/bypass HME; turn off humidifier if allowed.
- VCV mode, Vt 8–10 mL/kg, Ti > 1 s, Ti/Ttot ≥ 0.3–0.5.
- Gas flow 6–10 L/min to jet nebuliser.

**During:**

- Watch aerosol cloud, patient vitals, and circuit for leaks.

**After:**

- Stop when empty, remove nebuliser.
- Reconnect HME / humidifier.
- Restore baseline ventilator settings.
- Check expiratory filter, document response.

## Inhaled Antibiotics in ICU: Uses, Evidence, and Technical Know-How

Inhaled (aerosolised) antibiotics deliver very high local drug concentrations to the lungs while minimising systemic exposure, making them attractive for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and tracheobronchitis (VAT), especially with multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negatives. [^2_1][^2_2][^2_3][^2_4] However, current evidence supports **selective, not routine** use: mainly as **adjunctive or salvage therapy** for difficult pathogens, or as **prophylaxis** in high-risk ventilated patients. [^2_5][^2_6][^2_7][^2_4]

***

## 1. Why use inhaled antibiotics in ICU?

### Theoretical advantages

- **High local concentrations** in airways and alveoli, often many times above the MIC of the pathogen. [^2_3][^2_8]
- **Low systemic absorption** → less nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, and other systemic side effects. [^2_2][^2_9]
- Potential to **reduce emergence of resistance** by decreasing systemic antibiotic pressure. [^2_10][^2_3][^2_11]
- Can reach pathogens in **biofilms and secretions** that IV drugs may not penetrate well. [^2_3]


### Main limitations

- **Uneven distribution** in diseased lungs (atelectasis, consolidation, secretions). [^2_11][^2_8]
- **Device- and technique-dependent** delivery; suboptimal nebulisation can waste most of the dose. [^2_12][^2_8]
- Limited high-quality RCT evidence for **mortality benefit**; most data are on microbiological cure or VAP incidence. [^2_2][^2_5][^2_7][^2_4]

***

## 2. Commonly used inhaled antibiotics

### a) Colistin (polymyxin E)

- **Spectrum:** MDR Gram-negatives (e.g., *Pseudomonas*, *Acinetobacter*, some *Klebsiella*). [^2_3][^2_4]
- **Form:** Colistimethate sodium solution for nebulisation.
- **Use in ICU:**
    - Adjunctive therapy for VAP/VAT due to colistin-only-susceptible organisms.
    - Salvage when IV therapy fails or is too toxic. [^2_1][^2_6][^2_7]
- **Dosing (typical, varies by protocol):**
    - Often **1–2 million IU (≈ 40–80 mg colistin base activity)** nebulised every 12–24 h, in addition to IV therapy. [^2_3][^2_4]
- **Key points:**
    - Low systemic absorption; systemic toxicity rare but monitor for bronchospasm. [^2_3][^2_9]


### b) Aminoglycosides: Amikacin, Gentamicin, Tobramycin

- **Spectrum:** Gram-negatives including *Pseudomonas*. [^2_3][^2_13][^2_4]
- **Amikacin** is most studied for VAP prevention and treatment.
- **Use in ICU:**
    - **Prophylaxis:** Inhaled amikacin in high-risk ventilated patients reduces VAP incidence. [^2_14][^2_13][^2_4]
    - **Treatment:** Adjunctive therapy for MDR Gram-negative VAP/VAT, especially when IV options are limited. [^2_3][^2_7][^2_4]
- **Dosing (examples, protocol-dependent):**
    - Amikacin: **250–500 mg** nebulised every 12–24 h as adjunct. [^2_13][^2_4]
    - Tobramycin: **300 mg** nebulised every 12 h (more common in CF, adapted in some ICUs). [^2_3]
- **Key points:**
    - Very high local concentrations with minimal serum levels. [^2_3][^2_4]
    - Monitor for bronchospasm; pre-treat with bronchodilator if reactive airways. [^2_8]


### c) Other agents (less common, emerging)

- **Vancomycin:** Inhaled for MRSA VAP in some centres; evidence limited. [^2_3]
- **Fosfomycin, β-lactams:** Investigational; some prophylaxis studies (e.g., inhaled β-lactam reducing VAP incidence). [^2_3][^2_13]
- **Liposomal formulations:** Designed to improve lung retention; still largely research/early clinical use. [^2_8]

***

## 3. Clinical indications in ICU

### A. Prophylaxis (prevention of VAP/VAT)

- **Target group:**
    - Patients expected to be ventilated > 48–72 h, especially with high VAP risk (e.g., trauma, severe COPD, prior colonisation). [^2_14][^2_13][^2_4]
- **Regimen with strongest evidence:**
    - **Inhaled amikacin** (sometimes combined with other agents) in high-risk ventilated patients. [^2_13][^2_4]
- **Evidence:**
    - Multiple studies and meta-analyses show **significant reduction in VAP incidence** (RR ≈ 0.6–0.7). [^2_14][^2_13]
    - No consistent reduction in **ICU mortality** or **duration of ventilation**. [^2_14][^2_13]
- **Guideline stance:**
    - Some European/ICU groups consider it reasonable in selected high-risk patients; not universally adopted as standard of care. [^2_11][^2_4]

**Exam point:** “Inhaled amikacin prophylaxis reduces VAP incidence but not mortality.”

***

### B. Treatment of established VAP / VAT

- **Main indications:**
    - VAP/VAT caused by **MDR Gram-negative bacteria**, especially:
        - Only susceptible to aminoglycosides or polymyxins. [^2_1][^2_6][^2_7][^2_4]
    - **Failure of first-line IV therapy** (persistent fever, worsening infiltrates, ongoing positive cultures). [^2_1][^2_6][^2_7]
    - **Relapsing VAP** or infections with very high MICs. [^2_4]
    - Severely immunocompromised patients with difficult Gram-negative pneumonia. [^2_4]
- **How used:**
    - **Adjunctive** to appropriate IV antibiotics, not as monotherapy (except in very specific salvage scenarios under expert guidance). [^2_1][^2_6][^2_7][^2_4]
- **Evidence summary:**
    - **Microbiological eradication:** Consistently improved with adjunctive inhaled antibiotics vs IV alone. [^2_2][^2_7][^2_9]
    - **Clinical cure:** Some studies show improvement; others neutral. [^2_2][^2_7][^2_9]
    - **Mortality:** Most large RCTs (e.g., INHALE trial with amikacin) show **no mortality benefit**. [^2_7][^2_4]
    - **Safety:** Generally good; no clear increase in nephrotoxicity; bronchospasm is the main concern. [^2_3][^2_7][^2_9]
- **Guideline positions:**
    - **ATS/IDSA (USA):** Suggest inhaled antibiotics as **last-resort adjunct** for VAP due to organisms only susceptible to aminoglycosides/polymyxins. [^2_11][^2_9]
    - **ESCMID (Europe):** More cautious; many panels do **not recommend routine use** due to weak evidence and potential risks. [^2_11][^2_15]
    - Recent reviews: Consider on a **case-by-case basis** for high-risk or failing patients, particularly with MDR Gram-negatives. [^2_6][^2_7][^2_4]

**Exam one-liners:**

- “Inhaled antibiotics improve microbiological cure in VAP but do not reduce mortality.”
- “Indicated mainly for MDR Gram-negative VAP as adjunct or salvage.”

***

## 4. Technical know-how: how to give inhaled antibiotics on a ventilator

This is where most “real-world” failure happens. The drug must actually reach the lungs.

### A. Device selection

- **Vibrating Mesh Nebuliser (VMN)** – preferred for antibiotics. [^2_12][^2_7][^2_8]
    - High efficiency, low residual volume, can be left in circuit.
    - Better lung deposition than jet nebulisers.
- **Jet nebuliser** – acceptable if VMN unavailable, but more drug loss. [^2_12][^2_8]
- **Ultrasonic** – less commonly used for antibiotics in ICU.

**Exam tip:** “Best device for inhaled antibiotics in ventilated patient” → **Vibrating mesh**.

***

### B. Circuit set-up and positioning

- **Position:**
    - VMN: within **15 cm of the Y-piece** on the inspiratory limb. [^2_16][^2_7][^2_8]
    - Jet: **15–30 cm** from Y-piece on inspiratory limb. [^2_17][^2_18][^2_16]
- **Remove or bypass:**
    - **HME** between nebuliser and patient (reduces aerosol delivery by up to 40%). [^2_12]
    - Temporarily **turn off active heated humidifier** (“dry circuit”) to reduce particle growth and rain-out. [^2_12][^2_16][^2_8]
- **Expiratory filter:**
    - Place a **low-resistance bacterial/viral filter** on the expiratory limb to protect ventilator and environment. [^2_19][^2_20][^2_8]
    - Check for increased resistance post-treatment (filter clogging). [^2_12]

***

### C. Ventilator settings to optimise delivery

Goal: **slow, deep, long inspiratory flow** to allow aerosol to reach distal airways.

- **Mode:** Prefer **volume-controlled ventilation (VCV)** during treatment. [^2_16]
- **Tidal volume:** Temporarily increase to **8–10 mL/kg** if safe. [^2_16]
- **Inspiratory time (Ti):**
    - Aim **Ti > 1 s**, **Ti/Ttot ≥ 0.3–0.5**. [^2_18][^2_16][^2_21]
- **Flow:**
    - Use ventilator’s **nebuliser synchronisation** if available (diverts flow to power nebuliser during inspiration). [^2_12]
    - For jet: external gas flow **6–10 L/min**. [^2_18][^2_22]
- **RR:** May reduce slightly to prolong Ti (if safe). [^2_16]
- **PEEP:** Usually keep as prescribed; don’t routinely change. [^2_16]

***

### D. Drug preparation and administration

- **Dilution:**
    - Follow specific product/protocol; many antibiotics are given undiluted or with minimal saline to keep volume small but effective.
    - Total fill volume for jet nebulisers often **4–5 mL** (drug + saline) to ensure adequate misting time. [^2_18][^2_23][^2_19]
- **Pre-treatment:**
    - Suction secretions if heavy.
    - Consider **bronchodilator** before antibiotic in patients with reactive airways or prior bronchospasm. [^2_8]
- **Administration:**
    - Connect nebuliser to inspiratory limb at correct position.
    - Start nebulisation; ensure visible aerosol and stable circuit. [^2_12][^2_24]
    - Monitor SpO₂, HR, BP, airway pressures, and for signs of bronchospasm. [^2_22][^2_24][^2_8]
- **End of treatment:**
    - Stop when cup is nearly empty / no more mist.
    - Remove jet nebuliser (VMN may stay if protocol allows).
    - Reconnect HME and restart humidifier promptly to avoid mucus plugging. [^2_12][^2_16][^2_8]
    - Return ventilator settings to baseline.
    - Replace/check expiratory filter if resistance increased. [^2_12]

***

### E. Monitoring and safety

- **Clinical:**
    - Watch for **bronchospasm** (rise in peak pressure, wheeze, desaturation). [^2_8]
    - Monitor for improvement in fever, oxygenation, secretions, and radiographic changes over days.
- **Microbiological:**
    - Follow-up cultures (sputum/BAL) to document eradication where feasible. [^2_2][^2_7]
- **Systemic toxicity:**
    - Rare with inhaled aminoglycosides/colistin but still consider periodic renal function if prolonged courses or combined with IV nephrotoxins. [^2_3][^2_9]
- **Resistance:**
    - Theoretical concern of selecting resistant organisms in the airway; surveillance cultures and stewardship are important. [^2_10][^2_11]

***

## 5. Practical “checklist” style summary (exam-friendly)

**Indications to remember:**

- Prophylaxis: **high-risk ventilated patients** → inhaled **amikacin** reduces VAP incidence. [^2_14][^2_13][^2_4]
- Treatment:
    - **MDR Gram-negative VAP/VAT** (especially aminoglycoside- or colistin-only susceptible).
    - **Failure of IV therapy**, relapsing VAP, very high MIC, or immunocompromised host. [^2_1][^2_6][^2_7][^2_4]

**Key evidence points:**

- ↑ **Microbiological eradication** with adjunctive inhaled antibiotics. [^2_2][^2_7][^2_9]
- ± ↑ **Clinical cure** in some studies.
- **No clear mortality benefit** in large RCTs. [^2_2][^2_7][^2_4]
- Good **safety profile**; main risk is bronchospasm. [^2_3][^2_7][^2_9]

**Technical essentials:**

- Device: **Vibrating mesh** preferred. [^2_12][^2_7][^2_8]
- Position: **Close to Y-piece** on inspiratory limb (VMN closer than jet). [^2_16][^2_8]
- Remove **HME**, turn off or reduce **humidifier** during treatment. [^2_12][^2_16][^2_8]
- Optimise ventilator: **VCV, higher Vt, longer Ti, lower turbulence**. [^2_18][^2_16]
- Use **expiratory filter**; monitor for circuit resistance. [^2_12][^2_19][^2_8]
