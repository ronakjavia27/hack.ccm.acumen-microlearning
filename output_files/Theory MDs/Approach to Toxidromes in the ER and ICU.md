
## Approach to Toxidromes in the ER and ICU

Toxidromes are recognizable clinical syndromes produced by classes of poisons sharing similar mechanisms of toxicity. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3] Identifying the toxidrome at the bedside allows rapid initiation of targeted therapy (antidotes, decontamination, enhanced elimination) while awaiting confirmatory investigations, and is the cornerstone of management in both emergency and critical care settings. [^1_1][^1_4][^1_5]

***

## When to Suspect Poisoning and Apply the Toxidrome Framework

Apply the toxidrome approach to **any patient** presenting with: [^1_1]

- Altered conscious state or unexplained delirium
- Unexplained respiratory depression or failure
- Unexplained haemodynamic instability (shock, severe hypertension)
- Seizures without prior history
- Hyperthermia of unclear aetiology
- "Found-down" patients, agitated young adults post-recreational drug use, or polypharmacy overdoses

**Three governing principles:** [^1_1]

1. **Resuscitation precedes diagnosis** — secure ABCs and check bedside glucose before naming the toxidrome
2. **Constellation, not single signs** — individual signs overlap (e.g., miosis in opioids and pontine lesions; tachycardia in both sympathomimetic and anticholinergic toxidromes)
3. **Toxidrome as hypothesis generator** — directs early antidote, decontamination, and targeted investigations while history, drug levels, and ECG confirm the agent

***

## Initial Assessment and Resuscitation (ABCDE Approach)

### Airway

- At risk in any patient with depressed consciousness (GCS <8), vomiting, or seizures [^1_1][^1_6]
- **Intubate early** in deeply comatose patients before considering activated charcoal (aspiration risk) [^1_1][^1_6]


### Breathing

- Support with high-flow oxygen and assist ventilation as needed [^1_1][^1_6]
- **Respiratory rate <12/min or falling SpO₂ in a miotic patient** → naloxone trial: [^1_1]
    - Naloxone 400 mcg IV every 2–3 minutes, titrated to adequate ventilation (not full wakefulness)
    - 800 mcg IM if IV access absent
    - **Long-acting opioids (methadone, sustained-release oxycodone, fentanyl patches)** require naloxone infusion (start at two-thirds of the wakeful bolus per hour) to prevent re-narcotisation [^1_1][^1_7]


### Circulation

- IV fluids for vasodilated, hypotensive patients [^1_1]
- **Wide QRS (>100–120 ms) in suspected TCA overdose** → immediate sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mmol/kg IV bolus to overcome sodium-channel blockade; target pH 7.45–7.55 [^1_8][^1_1][^1_7]
- Avoid antiarrhythmics in TCA cardiotoxicity (worsen sodium channel blockade) [^1_1]


### Disability

- **Bedside glucose immediately** in every altered or seizing patient (hypoglycaemia is rapidly fatal and clinically silent in intoxicated patients) [^1_1][^1_6]
- Seizures: IV diazepam 10 mg or lorazepam 4 mg [^1_1]
- **Hyperthermia** — identify and treat aggressively with active cooling and benzodiazepines; antipyretics alone are ineffective [^1_1][^1_6]


### Exposure

- Look for needle-track marks, trauma, drug packets/pills, skin temperature, and moisture [^1_1]
- Check for odours (acetone, bitter almonds = cyanide, garlic = arsenic, etc.) [^1_7]

## The Six-Compartment Toxidrome Examination

Perform this **bedside examination in under 1 minute** and repeat serially (toxidromes evolve): [^1_1]


| Compartment | What to Assess | Key Discriminators |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Vital signs** | Temperature, HR, BP, RR, SpO₂ | Hyperthermia, hypo/hypertension, brady/tachypnoea |
| **Pupils** | Size, reactivity, nystagmus | Miosis (opioid, cholinergic, pontine) vs mydriasis (sympathomimetic, anticholinergic) |
| **Secretions** | Salivation, lacrimation, bronchorrhoea, diaphoresis | Excess = cholinergic/sympathomimetic; dry = anticholinergic/opioid |
| **Skin** | Temperature and moisture | Hot/dry = anticholinergic; hot/sweaty = sympathomimetic/serotonin |
| **Bowel sounds** | Hyperactive vs absent | Hyperactive = cholinergic; absent = anticholinergic/opioid |
| **Neuromuscular** | Tone, reflexes, inducible clonus | **Clonus = serotonin syndrome** (most specific sign); lead-pipe rigidity = NMS |

**Two high-yield findings:** [^1_1]

- **Inducible, sustained lower-limb clonus** → serotonin syndrome (separates from anticholinergic/sympathomimetic where reflexes are normal/depressed)
- **Bowel sounds** → hyperactive in cholinergic ("wet"); absent in anticholinergic/opioid ("dry")

## The Seven Classic Toxidromes

### 1. Anticholinergic Toxidrome

**Mechanism:** Muscarinic receptor blockade [^1_1][^1_7]

**Agents:** TCAs, antihistamines (diphenhydramine, promethazine), atropine, hyoscine, antiparkinsonian agents, plant poisons (deadly nightshade, jimsonweed) [^1_1][^1_7]

**Mnemonic:** "**Hot as a hare, dry as a bone, red as a beet, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter**" [^1_1][^1_7]


| Feature | Clinical Finding |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Temperature** | Hyperthermia with **anhidrosis** (sweat glands blocked) |
| **Skin** | Dry, flushed |
| **Pupils** | Mydriasis with cycloplegia (blurred near vision, photophobia) |
| **Mental state** | Agitated delirium, hallucinations |
| **Other** | Dry mouth, urinary retention, ileus (absent bowel sounds), tachycardia |

**Key discriminator from sympathomimetic:** **Dry skin** (anticholinergic) vs sweaty (sympathomimetic) [^1_1]

**Management:**

- Benzodiazepines for agitation and seizures
- Active cooling for hyperthermia
- **Physostigmine** (reversible cholinesterase inhibitor) in selected pure cases without QRS prolongation or co-ingestion [^1_1][^1_7]

***

### 2. Cholinergic Toxidrome

**Mechanism:** Acetylcholinesterase inhibition → acetylcholine accumulation at muscarinic, nicotinic, and CNS receptors [^1_1][^1_7]

**Agents:** Organophosphate/carbamate insecticides, nerve agents (sarin, VX), some mushroom poisons [^1_1][^1_7]

**Mnemonics:** "**SLUDGE**" and "**DUMBELS**" [^1_7]


| SLUDGE | DUMBELS |
| :-- | :-- |
| **S**alivation | **D**iarrhoea/Defecation |
| **L**acrimation | **U**rination |
| **U**rination | **M**iosis |
| **D**efecation/Diarrhoea | **B**radycardia/**B**ronchorrhoea/**B**ronchospasm |
| **G**I distress | **E**mesis |
| **E**mesis | **L**acrimation |
|  | **S**alivation/**S**eizures |

**Clinical features:** [^1_1][^1_7]

- **Wet patient:** copious oral/bronchial secretions, profuse sweating, lacrimation, salivation
- Miosis, bradycardia, bronchospasm, bronchorrhoea
- Hyperactive bowel sounds, diarrhoea, vomiting
- **Nicotinic effects:** muscle fasciculations → weakness → flaccid paralysis
- **Killer B's:** Bradycardia, Bronchorrhoea, Bronchoconstriction (immediate life threats) [^1_7]

**Management:** [^1_1][^1_7]

- **Decontamination** (remove clothing, wash skin) — protect healthcare workers
- **Atropine:** 0.02–0.04 mg/kg IV/IM (adult: 2 mg), double every 5 minutes until pulmonary secretions dry ("atropinisation"); then infusion (10–20% of total dose per hour)
- **Pralidoxime:** 30 mg/kg IV over 15 min (early, before irreversible enzyme "aging")
- Benzodiazepines for seizures
- Intubation with rocuronium (avoid succinylcholine — prolonged paralysis)

***

### 3. Sympathomimetic Toxidrome

**Mechanism:** Adrenergic excess (direct/indirect sympathomimetic activity) [^1_1]

**Agents:** Cocaine, amphetamine, methamphetamine, MDMA (ecstasy), decongestants (pseudoephedrine, phenylpropanolamine), caffeine, theophylline [^1_1]

**Clinical features:** [^1_1][^1_7]

- Hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthermia
- Mydriasis (with preserved accommodation)
- Diaphoresis (sweaty), agitation, tremor
- Piloerection ("gooseflesh"), brisk reflexes
- Psychosis, seizures

**Immediate dangers:** [^1_1]

- Hypertensive emergency, tachyarrhythmias
- Hyperthermia → rhabdomyolysis, DIC
- Intracranial haemorrhage (severe hypertension)

**Key discriminator from anticholinergic:** **Sweaty** (sympathomimetic) vs **dry** (anticholinergic) [^1_1]

**Management:**

- **Benzodiazepines** first-line for agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, seizures
- **Active cooling** for hyperthermia
- **Alpha-blockers** (phentolamine, nitroglycerin) for severe hypertension (avoid beta-blockers alone in cocaine toxicity — unopposed alpha stimulation)
- Avoid antipyretics (ineffective)

***

### 4. Opioid Toxidrome

**Mechanism:** Mu-receptor agonism at medullary respiratory centre [^1_1][^1_7]

**Agents:** Heroin, morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, methadone, tramadol, codeine [^1_1][^1_7]

**Classic triad:** [^1_1][^1_7]

1. CNS depression (coma)
2. **Respiratory depression** (reduced rate and tidal volume — most specific sign)
3. **Miosis** (pinpoint pupils)

**Other features:**

- Hypotension, hypothermia, bradycardia
- Normal skin, secretions, bowel sounds
- Drowsiness, conjunctival injection, euphoria (milder cases)

**Caveats:** [^1_7]

- Severe hypoxia from respiratory depression → pupillary dilation (misleading)
- Co-ingestion (e.g., stimulants) may mask typical features

**Management:** [^1_1][^1_7]

- **Naloxone:** 400 mcg IV every 2–3 min, titrated to adequate ventilation (not full wakefulness)
- **Naloxone infusion** for long-acting opioids (methadone, sustained-release formulations, fentanyl patches) — start at two-thirds of wakeful bolus per hour
- Airway protection, ventilation if needed
- Monitor for re-narcotisation (especially methadone)

***

### 5. Sedative-Hypnotic Toxidrome

**Mechanism:** GABA-A receptor potentiation (benzodiazepines, barbiturates, Z-drugs) or other CNS depressant effects [^1_1]

**Agents:** Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, zolpidem, zopiclone, ethanol, GHB [^1_1]

**Clinical features:** [^1_1]

- CNS depression (ataxia, dysarthria, slurred speech, descending consciousness)
- **Normal pupils** (no miosis or mydriasis)
- **Normal vital signs** (until massive overdose → respiratory depression)
- **Normal reflexes**
- No hyperthermia, no autonomic signs

**Key diagnostic clue:** **Normality** — absence of autonomic, pupillary, or temperature abnormalities in a comatose patient [^1_1]

**Management:**

- Supportive care (airway, ventilation if needed)
- **Flumazenil:** 200 mcg IV — **ONLY for isolated iatrogenic benzodiazepine oversedation** (e.g., post-procedural); **contraindicated** in unknown/co-ingested overdoses or chronic users (precipitates refractory seizures) [^1_1][^1_7]

***

### 6. Serotonin Syndrome

**Mechanism:** Excess central serotonergic activity [^1_1]

**Agents:** SSRI + another serotonergic agent (tramadol, linezolid, MAOI, pethidine, dextromethorphan, St John's wort, MDMA) [^1_1][^1_6]

**Triad:** [^1_1]

1. **Clonus** (spontaneous, inducible, or ocular) — **most specific sign**
2. **Hyperreflexia** (lower-limb predominant)
3. Autonomic instability with hyperthermia and agitation

**Clinical features:** [^1_1][^1_6]

- Onset rapid (within hours of dose change or new agent)
- Diaphoresis, mydriasis, tachycardia, hypertension
- Agitation, confusion, seizures (severe cases)
- Temperature >38.5°C, rigidity (severe disease)

**Key discriminators from anticholinergic/sympathomimetic:** **Clonus and hyperreflexia** (reflexes normal/depressed in other toxidromes) [^1_1]

**Management:** [^1_1][^1_6]

- **Stop all serotonergic agents**
- **Benzodiazepines** for agitation, seizures, muscle activity
- **Active cooling** for hyperthermia
- **Cyproheptadine:** 12 mg PO loading dose, then 2 mg every 2 hours (max 32 mg/24h) — serotonin antagonist
- Severe cases: paralysis and intubation for temperature control

***

### 7. Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)

**Mechanism:** Idiosyncratic dopamine D2 receptor antagonism [^1_1]

**Agents:** Antipsychotics (haloperidol, olanzapine, risperidone, clozapine), antiemetics (metoclopramide) [^1_1][^1_6]

**Tetrad:** [^1_1]

1. **Lead-pipe muscle rigidity** (generalized, severe)
2. **Hyperthermia**
3. **Altered mental state** (mutism, agitation, stupor)
4. **Autonomic instability** (labile BP, tachycardia, diaphoresis)

**Clinical features:** [^1_1][^1_6]

- Onset: days to weeks after starting/escalating antipsychotic
- **Hyporeflexia** (not hyperreflexia), **no clonus**
- Elevated CK, leukocytosis, transaminitis

**Key discriminators from serotonin syndrome:** [^1_1]

- **Rigidity** (NMS) vs **clonus/hyperreflexia** (serotonin syndrome)
- **Hyporeflexia** (NMS) vs **hyperreflexia** (serotonin syndrome)
- Slower onset (days–weeks for NMS vs hours for serotonin syndrome)

**Management:** [^1_1][^1_6]

- **Stop offending agent**
- **Benzodiazepines** for agitation
- **Active cooling**
- **Bromocriptine** (dopamine agonist) or **dantrolene** (direct muscle relaxant) in severe cases
- Supportive care (fluids, monitor for rhabdomyolysis, renal failure)

## Differential Diagnosis: Overlapping Toxidromes and Non-Toxic Mimics

### High-Stakes Distinctions

| Clinical Scenario | Key Differentiating Features |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Hot, dry, agitated patient** | Anticholinergic: dry skin, urinary retention, ileus, mydriasis with cycloplegia, no clonus |
| **Hot, sweaty, agitated patient** | Sympathomimetic: diaphoresis, piloerection, mydriasis with preserved accommodation, no clonus |
| **Hot, agitated with hyperreflexia** | Serotonin syndrome: **clonus**, hyperreflexia, rapid onset after serotonergic agent |
| **Hot, rigid, altered mental state** | NMS: **lead-pipe rigidity**, hyporeflexia, elevated CK, slower onset (days–weeks) |
| **Miotic, comatose patient** | Opioid: respiratory depression, responds to naloxone; Pontine haemorrhage/clonidine: no naloxone response, brainstem signs on imaging |

### Non-Toxic Mimics to Exclude

- **Hypoglycaemia** (check glucose immediately) [^1_1]
- Sepsis/encephalitis
- Thyrotoxic crisis
- Heat stroke
- Post-ictal state
- Structural intracranial lesions (pontine haemorrhage mimics opioid toxidrome but does not respond to naloxone) [^1_1]

## Investigations (Parallel with Resuscitation)

### Immediate Bedside Tests

- **Capillary glucose** — every altered or seizing patient [^1_1][^1_6]
- **12-lead ECG** — early in all suspected poisonings: [^1_1][^1_7]
    - TCA: widened QRS (>100 ms), right axis deviation, terminal R-wave >3 mm in aVR
    - QT prolongation (antipsychotics, methadone, some antidepressants)
    - Brady/tachyarrhythmias (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin)
- **Venous/arterial blood gas** — pH, lactate, anion gap, osmolar gap: [^1_1]
    - High anion-gap metabolic acidosis + high osmolar gap → toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol)
    - Respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis → early salicylate poisoning


### Directed Quantitative Levels (Send on ALL Deliberate Self-Poisoning)

- **Paracetamol level at 4 hours** (treatable, dose-dependent, initially asymptomatic) [^1_1][^1_7]
- **Salicylate level** [^1_1]
- Additional based on suspected agent: lithium, iron, digoxin, valproate, theophylline, methotrexate, toxic alcohol metabolites [^1_1]


### Other Investigations

- Renal function, electrolytes, liver enzymes, creatine kinase
- Beta-hCG in women of reproductive age
- **Qualitative urine toxicology screen:** slow, insensitive, rarely changes acute management — do not rely on it for resuscitation decisions [^1_1]

## Decontamination Strategies

### Activated Charcoal

- **Dose:** 50–100 g orally (1 g/kg in children) within **1 hour** of ingestion [^1_1][^1_6][^1_7]
- Adsorbs most organic poisons; ineffective for: lithium, iron, heavy metals, hydrocarbons, strong acids/alkalis, alcohols [^1_1][^1_7]
- **Contraindications:** [^1_7]
    - Unprotected airway (unless intubated) — aspiration causes severe pneumonitis
    - Caustics/corrosives, heavy metals, alcohols, rapid-absorption agents (cyanide, strychnine)
    - Low GCS, absent bowel sounds, obstruction, unstable patient


### Multiple-Dose Activated Charcoal (MDAC)

- **Dose:** 50 g every 4 hours [^1_1]
- Enhances elimination of drugs with enterohepatic circulation or "gut dialysis" effect: theophylline, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, quinine, dapsone, salicylates, TCAs, phenytoin [^1_1][^1_7]


### Whole Bowel Irrigation (WBI)

- **Solution:** Polyethylene glycol 1–2 L/hour (adults), 500 mL/hour (children) via NG tube until effluent clear [^1_1][^1_7]
- **Indications:** [^1_1][^1_7]
    - Iron, lithium overdoses (charcoal ineffective)
    - Sustained-release formulations
    - Body packers/stuffers
- **Contraindications:** haematemesis, ileus, obstruction, perforation [^1_7]


### Gastric Lavage

- **Vanishing role** — only within 1 hour of life-threatening ingestion in an **intubated** patient [^1_1][^1_7]
- Risks: perforation, aspiration, bradycardia
- Not recommended for routine use [^1_1][^1_7]


### Syrup of Ipecac

- **No longer recommended** in any setting [^1_1]

## Enhanced Elimination

### Haemodialysis

**Indications:** Severe poisoning with: [^1_1][^1_7]

- Lithium (acute >4 mmol/L; chronic >2.5 mmol/L)
- Salicylates (>100 mg/dL or >60 mg/dL with clinical/acid-base compromise)
- Metformin (severe acidosis, renal failure, haemodynamic instability)
- Toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol)
- Valproate, theophylline

**Pharmacological criteria for dialysability:** [^1_1]

- Water-soluble
- Low molecular weight
- Small volume of distribution
- Poorly protein-bound

**Mnemonic:** "**I STUMBLE**" — INH, Salicylates, Theophylline, Uremia, Methanol, Barbiturates, Lithium, Ethylene glycol [^1_7]

### Urine Alkalinisation

- **IV sodium bicarbonate** to achieve urine pH ≥7.5 [^1_1]
- Enhances renal excretion of salicylates (alternative to dialysis in moderate cases) [^1_1]

| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |

| **Paracetamol** | N-acetylcysteine (NAC) | 150 mg/kg over 1h → 50 mg/kg over 4h → 100 mg/kg over 16h (21h regimen) | Safe in pregnancy; use nomogram at 4h [^1_1][^1_7] |

## ICU-Specific Considerations

### Indications for ICU Admission

- Intubation and mechanical ventilation for decreased GCS, respiratory compromise [^1_8][^1_6][^1_7]
- Cardiovascular instability (shock, severe arrhythmias, need for vasopressors) [^1_8][^1_6]
- Severe hyperthermia requiring active cooling and paralysis [^1_6]
- Need for continuous antidote infusions (naloxone, atropine, bicarbonate, NAC) [^1_8][^1_7]
- Enhanced elimination (haemodialysis, MDAC, WBI) [^1_1][^1_6]
- Long-acting/sustained-release ingestions requiring prolonged monitoring [^1_8][^1_5]
- Polypharmacy overdoses with evolving/mixed toxidromes [^1_1]


### ICU Monitoring and Management

- **Continuous cardiac monitoring** — arrhythmias (TCA, antipsychotics, methadone) [^1_8][^1_6]
- **Serial toxidrome reassessment** — toxidromes evolve (sympathomimetic → serotonin syndrome) [^1_1]
- **Temperature monitoring** — aggressive cooling for hyperthermia (ice packs, cold IV fluids, evaporative cooling, paralysis if needed) [^1_6]
- **CK monitoring** — rhabdomyolysis risk (sympathomimetics, serotonin syndrome, NMS, prolonged seizures) [^1_6][^1_7]
- **Acid-base and electrolyte monitoring** — metabolic acidosis (salicylates, toxic alcohols, metformin), hyperkalaemia (rhabdomyolysis, digoxin) [^1_1][^1_6]
- **Neurological monitoring** — seizures, delirium, neuroprognostication post-cardiac arrest (if hypoxic injury) [^1_6]


### Extracorporeal Treatments in Poisoning

Consider extracorporeal treatment (haemodialysis, haemoperfusion, ECMO) if: [^1_4][^1_6]

- Exposure likely to cause serious morbidity/mortality
- Toxicity unlikely to be prevented/reversed by antidote
- Toxicity unlikely to be minimized by decontamination/enhanced elimination
- Poison's endogenous clearance <4 mL/min/kg

**ECMO** for severe circulatory failure refractory to maximal medical therapy (e.g., calcium channel blocker, beta-blocker overdose) [^1_6]

## Special Populations

### Children

- Unpredictable ingestion quantities ("handful at grandmother's house") [^1_1]
- **Weight-based dosing essential:** charcoal 1 g/kg, naloxone 100 mcg/kg, all antidotes [^1_1]
- Lower threshold for intubation before charcoal (aspiration risk) [^1_1]


### Pregnant Patients

- **Mother is primary patient** — resuscitate aggressively [^1_1]
- NAC and charcoal are safe in pregnancy; paracetamol nomogram applies unchanged [^1_1]
- Avoid flumazenil [^1_1]
- Intentional overdose is a leading cause of maternal death — requires psychosocial assessment [^1_1]


### Body Packers/Stuffers

- **Body packer:** manufactured drug packets (professional smugglers) — risk of rupture/obstruction → surgical emergency [^1_1]
- **Body stuffer:** loose drugs swallowed to avoid arrest — unpredictable absorption
- **Management:** [^1_1]
    - Asymptomatic: whole bowel irrigation + observation
    - Symptomatic (abdominal pain, obstruction, toxicity): surgical consultation


### Elderly and Polypharmacy Patients

- Mixed or attenuated toxidromes (anticholinergic burden blurs signs) [^1_1]
- Reduced physiological reserve → lower threshold for ICU admission [^1_1]
- Chronic overdoses (methadone accumulation, lithium toxicity in dehydration) develop over days [^1_1]

## Common Pitfalls and Errors

| Pitfall | Consequence | Prevention |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Relying on qualitative urine toxicology screen for acute decisions | Delayed/incorrect management | Use toxidrome + targeted levels; urine screen is slow/insensitive [^1_1] |
| Giving flumazenil in unknown/co-ingested overdose | Refractory seizures (esp. with TCA, theophylline, cocaine) | Reserve for isolated iatrogenic benzodiazepine oversedation [^1_1][^1_7] |
| Administering charcoal after 1 hour or to unprotected airway | Ineffective; aspiration pneumonitis | Give within 1 hour; intubate first or withhold [^1_1][^1_6][^1_7] |
| Forgetting paracetamol/salicylate levels in deliberate self-poisoning | Missed treatable toxicity | Send on ALL deliberate overdoses regardless of history [^1_1][^1_7] |
| Failing to recognise evolving toxidrome | Delayed appropriate therapy | Serial reassessment; sympathomimetic + clonus = serotonin syndrome [^1_1] |
| Treating hyperthermia with antipyretics alone | Ineffective; worsening outcomes | Active cooling + benzodiazepines + cyproheptadine (serotonin syndrome) [^1_1][^1_6] |
| Under-dosing atropine in organophosphate poisoning | Persistent bronchorrhoea, respiratory failure | Dose in milligrams; repeat until chest dries; infusion to maintain [^1_1][^1_7] |
| Delaying haemodialysis in severe salicylate/lithium/metformin toxicity | Irreversible harm/death | Early nephrology consultation; know dialysis triggers [^1_1][^1_7] |
| Normal early paracetamol level (<4 hours) interpreted as "safe" | Missed hepatotoxicity | Repeat at 4 hours post-ingestion; use nomogram [^1_1] |
| Using antiarrhythmics in TCA cardiotoxicity | Worsens sodium channel blockade | Sodium bicarbonate is first-line; avoid Class Ia/Ic antiarrhythmics [^1_1][^1_7] |


***

## Disposition

- **Home:** stable, minimal ingestion, reliable follow-up; provide written information on warning signs [^1_8][^1_7]
- **Psychiatric referral:** intentional overdose or at-risk patients [^1_8][^1_7]
- **Admission:** unstable, long-acting/sustained-release ingestions, need for observation [^1_8][^1_5][^1_7]
- **ICU:** intubation/ventilation, cardiovascular/respiratory compromise, severe hyperthermia, need for continuous antidotes/enhanced elimination [^1_8][^1_6][^1_7]

## Topic Pearls

- **Clonus is the hallmark of serotonin syndrome** — inducible, sustained lower-limb clonus with hyperreflexia distinguishes it from anticholinergic/sympathomimetic toxidromes. [^1_1]
- **Dry vs sweaty skin** separates anticholinergic (dry, anhidrotic, urinary retention, ileus) from sympathomimetic (diaphoretic, piloerection) toxidromes. [^1_1]
- **Flumazenil is dangerous** in unknown/co-ingested overdoses — precipitates refractory seizures; reserve for isolated iatrogenic benzodiazepine oversedation. [^1_1][^1_7]
- **Wide QRS in TCA overdose = sodium bicarbonate** (1–2 mmol/kg IV), not antiarrhythmics — treats sodium-channel blockade. [^1_8][^1_1][^1_7]
- **Dialyse "I STUMBLE"** — INH, Salicylates, Theophylline, Uremia, Methanol, Barbiturates, Lithium, Ethylene glycol. [^1_7]
- **Every deliberate self-poisoning needs paracetamol and salicylate levels** — treatable, dose-dependent, initially asymptomatic. [^1_1][^1_7]
- **Long-acting opioids re-narcotise** — naloxone infusion required for methadone, sustained-release oxycodone, fentanyl patches. [^1_1][^1_7]
- **Hyperthermia with rigidity is life-threatening** — cool, sedate, and treat the cause (cyproheptadine for serotonin syndrome; bromocriptine/dantrolene for NMS); antipyretics alone are ineffective. [^1_1][^1_6]
