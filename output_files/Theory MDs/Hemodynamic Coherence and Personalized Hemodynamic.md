

## Hemodynamic Coherence and Personalized Hemodynamic Management in Critically Ill Patients

**Hemodynamic coherence** describes the physiological coupling between the macrocirculation (systemic hemodynamics) and microcirculation (tissue-level perfusion). When coherence is preserved, improvements in systemic parameters (e.g., blood pressure, cardiac output) parallel restoration of microvascular flow and cellular oxygenation. **Loss of hemodynamic coherence** (hemodynamic incoherence) occurs when macrocirculatory resuscitation fails to restore microcirculatory perfusion—a dissociation strongly associated with organ dysfunction and mortality, particularly in sepsis. [^1][^2][^3][^4]

**Personalized hemodynamic management** shifts from fixed numerical targets (e.g., MAP ≥65 mmHg for all) to individualized resuscitation guided by dynamic assessment of tissue perfusion, fluid responsiveness, and patient-specific hemodynamic profiles. The 2025 ESICM guidelines and the ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 trial provide high-level evidence supporting perfusion-guided, phenotype-based strategies over protocolized, one-size-fits-all approaches. [^5][^6][^7][^8]

***

## 1. Definition and Conceptual Framework

### 1.1 Hemodynamic Coherence

- **Definition:** Hemodynamic coherence exists when resuscitation procedures aimed at correcting systemic hemodynamic variables effectively restore regional and microcirculatory perfusion and oxygen delivery, enabling parenchymal cells to perform functional activities supporting organ function. [^2]
- **Clinical implication:** Normalizing blood pressure or cardiac output does **not** guarantee microcirculatory recovery. Persistent microvascular dysfunction despite corrected macrohemodynamics defines **hemodynamic incoherence**. [^3][^4]


### 1.2 Hemodynamic Incoherence (Loss of Coherence)

- **Pathophysiology:** Shock-induced endothelial dysfunction, glycocalyx degradation, heterogeneous capillary perfusion, impaired vasoregulation, increased vascular permeability, and microthrombus formation lead to microcirculatory dysfunction that is dissociated from systemic parameters. [^4][^9]
- **Clinical phenotype:** Patients may have "normalized" MAP, CO, and lactate but exhibit persistent skin mottling, prolonged capillary refill time (CRT), or abnormal sublingual microcirculatory flow. [^10][^3]


### 1.3 Temporal Dissociation

- Microcirculatory deterioration **precedes** macrocirculatory changes in early shock (e.g., endotoxemic shock models show sublingual microvascular flow reduction at 30 min, while MAP drops at 90–120 min). [^11]
- During resuscitation, macrocirculatory parameters may normalize while microcirculatory perfusion remains impaired—explaining persistent organ failure despite "successful" resuscitation. [^3][^12]

## 2. Pathophysiology of Hemodynamic Incoherence

### 2.1 Endothelial Glycocalyx Degradation

- **Structure:** The glycocalyx is a gel-like layer (heparan sulfate, syndecan-1, hyaluronan) covering the endothelial luminal surface, regulating vascular permeability, shear stress transduction, leukocyte adhesion, and coagulation. [^13][^14]
- **Sepsis-induced damage:**
    - Inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6), reactive oxygen species, and sheddases (heparanase, MMPs, ADAMs) degrade glycocalyx components. [^13][^14]
    - Circulating glycocalyx fragments (syndecan-1, heparan sulfate) correlate with organ failure severity and mortality. [^13][^9]
- **Consequences:**
    - Increased capillary permeability → interstitial edema.
    - Impaired mechanotransduction → abnormal vasomotor tone.
    - Exposed adhesion molecules → leukocyte/platelet aggregation → microthrombosis. [^4][^9]


### 2.2 Microcirculatory Alterations in Sepsis

- **Key features:**
    - Decreased functional capillary density.
    - Increased heterogeneity of blood flow (perfused capillaries adjacent to non-perfused ones).
    - Increased proportion of stopped/intermittently perfused capillaries. [^10][^4]
- **Mechanisms:**
    - Endothelial activation and dysfunction.
    - Impaired autoregulation.
    - Red blood cell deformability reduction.
    - Leukocyte-endothelial interactions. [^10][^15]


### 2.3 Types of Hemodynamic Incoherence

| Type | Description | Clinical Context |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Type 1: Heterogeneous perfusion** | Patchy capillary flow with perfused and non-perfused capillaries coexisting | Septic shock; associated with poor prognosis [^4] |
| **Type 2: Vasoplegic incoherence** | Impaired vasomotor response despite adequate systemic pressure | Distributive shock with persistent microvascular dysfunction |
| **Type 3: Venous congestion** | Elevated venous pressure impairs microcirculatory flow despite normal arterial pressure | Cardiogenic shock, fluid overload, right heart failure [^4] |

## 3. Clinical Assessment of Hemodynamic Coherence

### 3.1 Macrocirculatory Monitoring (Standard)

- **Parameters:** MAP, heart rate, cardiac output/stroke volume, central venous pressure (CVP), ScvO₂, lactate.
- **Limitations:** Poor correlation with microcirculatory perfusion; normal values do not exclude tissue hypoxia. [^2][^3]


### 3.2 Microcirculatory and Tissue Perfusion Monitoring

| Parameter | Method | Clinical Utility | Limitations |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Capillary Refill Time (CRT)** | Manual compression of distal phalanx (10 sec), time to color return | Simple, bedside, prognostic; target <3 sec | Operator-dependent, affected by ambient temperature, anemia [^16][^7] |
| **Skin mottling score** | Visual assessment of knee/thigh mottling (0–5 scale) | Strong predictor of mortality in septic shock | Subjective, less sensitive in early shock |
| **Skin temperature gradient** | Central-to-peripheral temperature difference | Reflects peripheral vasoconstriction | Affected by external warming, sedation |
| **Sublingual video microscopy** | Sidestream Dark Field (SDF) / Incident Dark Field (IDF) imaging | Direct visualization of microvascular flow index (MFI), perfused vessel density (PVD) | Research tool, requires expertise, not routine [^12] |
| **Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS)** | Tissue oxygen saturation (StO₂) | Non-invasive, continuous | Affected by skin pigmentation, edema |
| **Veno-arterial PCO₂ gap (P(v-a)CO₂)** | Central venous and arterial PCO₂ difference | Marker of adequacy of cardiac output relative to metabolism; >6 mmHg suggests low flow | Requires central line, affected by respiratory status [^6][^17] |
| **Lactate kinetics** | Serial arterial lactate | Marker of global tissue hypoxia; clearance >10%/hr associated with better outcomes | Lag time, confounded by liver dysfunction, type B lactate |

### 3.3 ESICM 2025 Recommendations on Perfusion Monitoring

- **Skin perfusion** should be monitored using CRT assessment, complemented by skin temperature and mottling (UGPS—ungraded good practice statement). [^6]
- In patients with a central venous catheter, **serial ScvO₂** and **P(v-a)CO₂** measurements should be performed (UGPS). [^6]
- **Blood lactate** should be measured in all suspected shock cases and trended serially. [^6][^17]

## 4. Personalized Hemodynamic Management: Principles and Evidence

### 4.1 Core Principles

1. **Individualize blood pressure targets:**
    - Consider baseline blood pressure, chronic hypertension, and organ autoregulation thresholds.
    - Avoid both under- and over-resuscitation. [^18][^19][^20]
2. **Assess fluid responsiveness dynamically:**
    - Use dynamic indices (PLR, PPV, SVV) rather than static preload markers (CVP, PAOP). [^6][^17][^21]
3. **Target tissue perfusion, not just pressure:**
    - Use CRT, lactate clearance, ScvO₂, and P(v-a)CO₂ to guide resuscitation adequacy. [^16][^6][^7]
4. **Phenotype-driven therapy:**
    - Tailor fluids, vasopressors, and inotropes based on hemodynamic profile (e.g., low CO vs. high CO, fluid responsive vs. non-responsive). [^16][^7]

### 4.2 Fluid Responsiveness Assessment

| Test | Indication | Threshold for Responsiveness | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Passive Leg Raise (PLR)** | All shock states; preferred in spontaneous breathing or low tidal volume | ΔSV or ΔCO ≥10–15% | Reversible, safe; use echocardiography (LVOT VTI) or pulse pressure as surrogate [^22][^23][^24] |
| **Pulse Pressure Variation (PPV)** | Mechanically ventilated, regular rhythm, tidal volume ≥8 mL/kg | PPV ≥12–13% | Limited in low tidal volume, arrhythmias, spontaneous breathing [^21][^25] |
| **Stroke Volume Variation (SVV)** | Mechanically ventilated with arterial waveform monitoring | SVV ≥10–12% | Same limitations as PPV [^21] |
| **ΔPPV during PLR** | Low tidal volume ventilation | ΔPPV ≥2–3% | Overcomes PPV limitations in low VT; high accuracy (AUC 0.92) [^23][^26] |
| **End-expiratory occlusion test** | Mechanically ventilated | ΔCO ≥5% over 15 sec | Requires CO monitoring |

**Key evidence:**

- PLR-induced changes in PPV (ΔPPV_PLR) accurately predict fluid responsiveness even in low tidal volume ventilation (AUC 0.92, grey zone 4.8%). [^23][^26]
- Static markers (CVP, PAOP) should **not** be used alone to guide fluid resuscitation. [^17]


### 4.3 ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 Trial (2025): CRT-Guided Personalized Resuscitation

**Study design:**

- International, multicenter RCT (n≈2,700) in early septic shock.
- **Intervention:** Personalized hemodynamic resuscitation targeting CRT normalization (CRT-PHR) using a 6-hour stepwise algorithm.
- **Control:** Usual care (lactate-guided or clinician discretion).
- **Primary outcome:** Hierarchical composite of 28-day mortality, duration of vital support, and hospital length of stay. [^7][^8][^27]

**CRT-PHR Algorithm (simplified):** [^16]

1. **Abnormal CRT (>3 sec)?**
    - Yes → Assess fluid responsiveness (PLR).
    - If responsive → Fluid challenge until CRT normalizes or non-responsive.
    - If non-responsive → Check pulse pressure <40 mmHg or diastolic BP <50 mmHg.
        - If yes → Vasopressor titration.
        - If no → Assess cardiac dysfunction (echocardiography).
            - If cardiac dysfunction → Inotrope (dobutamine).
            - If prior hypertension → Higher MAP target (e.g., 75–85 mmHg).

**Results:**

- **Primary outcome:** Win ratio 1.16 (95% CI 1.02–1.33; P=0.04) favoring CRT-PHR.
- **28-day mortality:** No significant difference (26.5% CRT-PHR vs. 26.6% usual care; HR 0.99). [^7][^8]
- **Duration of vital support:** Significantly shorter in CRT-PHR group (driven the composite benefit).
- **Interpretation:** CRT-guided personalized resuscitation reduced organ support duration without mortality benefit, supporting tissue perfusion as a valid resuscitation target. [^7][^28][^29]

**Clinical implications:**

- CRT is a practical, bedside marker of microcirculatory perfusion that can guide resuscitation.
- Personalized, phenotype-based algorithms outperform usual care in reducing organ support, even if mortality is unchanged.
- Supports 2025 ESICM emphasis on perfusion-guided (not pressure-only) resuscitation. [^5][^6]


### 4.4 ESICM 2025 Guidelines on Circulatory Shock and Hemodynamic Monitoring

**Key recommendations relevant to personalized management:** [^5][^6][^19]


| Domain | Recommendation | Strength |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Initial assessment** | Echocardiography as first-line imaging to determine shock type | Graded statement |
| **Fluid resuscitation** | Assess fluid responsiveness with dynamic indices before continuing fluids in persistent shock | UGPS |
| **Fluid responsiveness** | Use dynamic variables (PLR, PPV, SVV) over static markers (CVP, PAOP) | Graded statement |
| **CO monitoring** | Monitor CO/SV in patients not responding to initial therapy | UGPS |
| **Arterial pressure** | Invasive arterial monitoring in shock unresponsive to initial therapy or requiring vasopressors | UGPS |
| **Blood pressure target** | Individualize MAP target during resuscitation (consider baseline BP, comorbidities) | UGPS |
| **Tissue perfusion** | Monitor skin perfusion (CRT, mottling, temperature); serial ScvO₂ and P(v-a)CO₂ if CVC in situ | UGPS |
| **Lactate** | Measure and trend lactate in all suspected shock | UGPS |

**Specific MAP targets:**

- **General septic shock:** Individualize; MAP ≥65 mmHg is a starting point, but higher targets may be needed in chronic hypertension. [^19]
- **Trauma without TBI:** MAP 50–60 mmHg (SBP 80–90 mmHg) until bleeding controlled. [^19]
- **Traumatic brain injury (GCS ≤8):** MAP ≥80 mmHg initially. [^19]
- **Cardiogenic shock:** MAP ≥65 mmHg may be considered; individualize based on organ perfusion. [^19]

## 5. Practical Approach to Personalized Hemodynamic Management

### 5.1 Initial Resuscitation Algorithm (First 6 Hours)

```
Step 1: Recognize shock
  - Clinical signs (hypotension, altered mental status, oliguria, mottling, prolonged CRT)
  - Lactate ≥2 mmol/L or rising

Step 2: Immediate stabilization
  - Secure airway, oxygen, IV access
  - Start balanced crystalloids (e.g., 30 mL/kg if septic shock)
  - Obtain arterial and central venous access if shock persists

Step 3: Phenotype the shock
  - Bedside echocardiography: assess LV/RV function, IVC, pericardium
  - Determine: hypovolemic, distributive, cardiogenic, obstructive

Step 4: Assess fluid responsiveness
  - PLR test with LVOT VTI or pulse pressure change
  - If responsive → Fluid challenge (250–500 mL crystalloid)
  - Reassess CRT, lactate, CO, MAP after each bolus
  - Stop if: CRT normalizes, fluid responsiveness negative, or safety limits reached (e.g., CVP >18 mmHg, worsening oxygenation)

Step 5: If still in shock (MAP <65 mmHg or CRT >3 sec or lactate rising)
  - Start norepinephrine (first-line vasopressor)
  - Target individualized MAP (consider baseline BP, chronic HTN)
  - Reassess tissue perfusion (CRT, lactate, ScvO₂, P(v-a)CO₂)

Step 6: If persistent shock despite fluids + norepinephrine
  - Monitor CO/SV (echocardiography, pulse contour, or PAC if needed)
  - If low CO with adequate preload → Consider inotrope (dobutamine)
  - If high CO with persistent hypotension → Consider vasopressin or angiotensin II
  - If elevated P(v-a)CO₂ (>6 mmHg) with low CO → Optimize CO further

Step 7: Reassess every 15–30 min
  - CRT, MAP, lactate trend, urine output, mental status
  - Adjust therapy based on perfusion response, not just MAP
```


### 5.2 Hemodynamic Phenotypes and Targeted Therapy

| Phenotype | Hemodynamic Profile | CRT | Lactate | Therapy |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Hypovolemic** | Low CO, low SVV/PPV if responsive | Prolonged | Elevated | Fluids (if responsive), no vasopressor initially |
| **Distributive (septic)** | High/normal CO, low SVR, fluid responsive early | Prolonged | Elevated | Fluids (dynamic assessment), norepinephrine, consider vasopressin |
| **Cardiogenic** | Low CO, high SVR, elevated filling pressures | Prolonged | Elevated | Inotropes (dobutamine, milrinone), cautious fluids, consider mechanical support |
| **Obstructive (PE, tamponade)** | Low CO, high SVR, RV strain or pericardial effusion | Prolonged | Elevated | Treat underlying cause (thrombolysis, pericardiocentesis) |
| **Venous congestion** | Normal/high CO, elevated CVP, organ edema | Variable | Variable | Diuresis, CRRT, reduce fluids, optimize venous return |

## 6. Monitoring Tools and Technologies

### 6.1 Minimally Invasive CO Monitoring

- **Pulse contour analysis (e.g., LiDCO, PiCCO, FloTrac):**
    - Provides continuous CO, SV, SVV, PPV.
    - Requires arterial line; calibration may be needed.
    - Useful for dynamic assessment and fluid titration.
- **Echocardiography:**
    - LVOT VTI × heart rate × cross-sectional area = CO.
    - First-line for shock phenotyping and fluid responsiveness (PLR-induced ΔVTI ≥10–15%). [^6][^30]
    - Serial assessments guide therapy response.


### 6.2 Advanced Monitoring (Refractory Shock)

- **Pulmonary artery catheter (PAC):**
    - Consider in complex shock (combined cardiogenic + distributive, RV failure, unclear etiology).
    - Provides CO, SvO₂, PAOP, PVR.
    - ESICM 2025: Transpulmonary thermodilution or PAC may be considered when CO monitoring is required. [^19]
- **Tissue oxygenation monitoring:**
    - NIRS (StO₂) for regional oxygenation (e.g., thenar muscle).
    - Research/emerging use; not yet routine.

## 7. Complications and Pitfalls

### 7.1 Over-Resuscitation

- **Risks:**
    - Fluid overload → tissue edema, impaired oxygen diffusion, abdominal compartment syndrome, AKI, prolonged ventilation.
    - Excessive vasopressors → digital/visceral ischemia, arrhythmias.
- **Prevention:**
    - Use dynamic indices to avoid fluid in non-responders.
    - De-resuscitate early (diuretics, CRRT) once shock resolves.


### 7.2 Under-Resuscitation

- **Risks:**
    - Persistent tissue hypoxia → organ failure, death.
- **Prevention:**
    - Serial perfusion assessment (CRT, lactate, ScvO₂).
    - Escalate monitoring (CO, echocardiography) if not responding.


### 7.3 Misinterpretation of Markers

| Marker | Pitfall | Solution |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Lactate** | Type B lactate (liver failure, metformin, seizures) | Trend lactate; interpret with clinical context |
| **CRT** | Cold environment, anemia, vasoconstrictors | Standardize measurement; use with other perfusion markers |
| **ScvO₂** | High ScvO₂ in sepsis (microcirculatory shunt, mitochondrial dysfunction) | Combine with P(v-a)CO₂ and lactate |
| **PPV/SVV** | Arrhythmias, low tidal volume, spontaneous breathing | Use PLR or ΔPPV_PLR instead |

## 8. Prognostic Value of Hemodynamic Coherence

- **Microcirculatory parameters** (MFI, PVD, CRT) independently predict mortality and organ failure in sepsis, even after adjusting for macrohemodynamics. [^10][^15]
- **Persistent hemodynamic incoherence** (normal MAP/CO but abnormal CRT/microcirculation) is associated with:
    - Higher SOFA scores.
    - Prolonged mechanical ventilation.
    - Increased 28-day and ICU mortality. [^3][^4]
- **CRT normalization within 6 hours** correlates with better outcomes in septic shock (ANDROMEDA-SHOCK, ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2). [^7][^8]

## 10. Key Clinical Pearls (Exam-Oriented)

1. **Hemodynamic coherence** = macrocirculatory improvement parallels microcirculatory recovery; loss of coherence = organ failure despite normal BP/CO. [^2][^3]
2. **Glycocalyx degradation** is central to sepsis-induced microcirculatory dysfunction; measured by syndecan-1, heparan sulfate levels (research). [^13][^14]
3. **CRT >3 seconds** is abnormal and predicts mortality in septic shock; target CRT normalization in resuscitation (ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2). [^16][^7]
4. **Fluid responsiveness** must be assessed dynamically (PLR, PPV, SVV); static markers (CVP, PAOP) are unreliable. [^6][^17][^21]
5. **PLR test** is the most versatile: works in spontaneous breathing, low tidal volume, arrhythmias; use ΔLVOT VTI or ΔPPV. [^23][^24][^26]
6. **ESICM 2025:** Echocardiography is first-line for shock phenotyping; CO monitoring reserved for non-responders. [^6][^19]
7. **Individualize MAP targets:** Chronic hypertensives may need MAP 75–85 mmHg; trauma without TBI can tolerate MAP 50–60 mmHg. [^19]
8. **P(v-a)CO₂ >6 mmHg** suggests inadequate CO relative to metabolism; use with ScvO₂ to guide inotrope/fluid therapy. [^6][^17]
9. **Lactate clearance >10%/hour** is associated with better outcomes; use as a trend, not a single value. [^11][^3]
10. **ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2:** CRT-guided personalized resuscitation reduced organ support duration but not mortality—supports perfusion-guided care. [^7][^8]

***

## 11. Unresolved Questions and Future Directions

- **Routine microcirculation monitoring:** Sublingual video microscopy remains a research tool; cost-effectiveness and impact on mortality unclear. [^12]
- **Biomarkers of glycocalyx injury:** Syndecan-1, heparan sulfate as therapeutic targets (e.g., glycocalyx protection strategies) under investigation. [^13][^14]
- **Optimal MAP in chronic hypertension:** Trials ongoing (e.g., SEPSIS-PAM, individualized MAP trials); no definitive mortality benefit yet for higher targets. [^19]
- **AI-driven hemodynamic management:** Machine learning algorithms integrating continuous CO, CRT, lactate, and echocardiography for real-time decision support—emerging field. [^31]
