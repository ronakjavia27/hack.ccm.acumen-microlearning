
## Critical Care Neuromyopathy (ICU-Acquired Weakness)

Critical care neuromyopathy—encompassing critical illness polyneuropathy (CIP), critical illness myopathy (CIM), and their overlap (CIPNM)—is the commonest cause of new-onset limb and respiratory muscle weakness in ICU survivors, strongly associated with sepsis, multiorgan failure, ARDS, and prolonged ventilation. It presents as flaccid weakness and difficult weaning, is diagnosed by clinical criteria plus electrophysiology (and sometimes biopsy), and managed primarily by prevention, risk-factor control, and early rehabilitation since no disease-specific drug therapy exists. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3][^1_4]

## Definitions and Terminology

- **Critical Illness Polyneuropathy (CIP):** Acute axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy occurring in critically ill patients, typically with sepsis/MOF. [^1_1][^1_5]
- **Critical Illness Myopathy (CIM):** Acute primary myopathy with muscle inexcitability and myosin loss, often linked to steroids and neuromuscular blockers. [^1_1][^1_6]
- **Critical Illness Polyneuromyopathy (CIPNM):** Overlap syndrome with features of both CIP and CIM; clinically most patients have mixed pathology. [^1_1][^1_2]
- **ICU-Acquired Weakness (ICUAW):** Clinical umbrella term for new weakness in ICU; CIP/CIM are the main substrates. [^1_2][^1_3]


## Epidemiology and Clinical Impact

- Affects roughly **50–80%** of patients with prolonged ICU stay and sepsis/MOF/ARDS; incidence rises with severity and duration of critical illness. [^1_2][^1_3]
- Strongly associated with **prolonged mechanical ventilation**, weaning failure, tracheostomy, longer ICU/hospital stay, higher mortality, and long-term functional disability. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_4]


## Etiology and Risk Factors

Multifactorial; sepsis-driven inflammation is central, with microvascular, metabolic, and iatrogenic contributors. [^1_2][^1_7]

### Major predisposing conditions

- **Sepsis / SIRS / Multiorgan dysfunction** (strongest and most consistent risk factor) [^1_8][^1_2][^1_7]
- **ARDS**, severe pneumonia, acute respiratory failure [^1_5][^1_2]
- **Prolonged ICU stay**, immobility, bed rest [^1_2][^1_7]


### Iatrogenic and metabolic contributors

- **High-dose corticosteroids** (especially IV methylprednisolone) [^1_5][^1_7][^1_9]
- **Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs)**, particularly prolonged infusions [^1_5][^1_7][^1_10]
- **Aminoglycosides** and other potentially neurotoxic drugs [^1_5]
- **Hyperglycemia** and glycemic variability; intensive insulin therapy reduces CIP incidence in some studies [^1_7][^1_4]
- **Electrolyte disturbances**: hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia (can mimic or worsen weakness) [^1_11]
- **Acidosis**, hepatic failure, post-transplant states (lung, liver) [^1_5][^1_10]


## Pathophysiology (Exam-Relevant Mechanisms)

- **Systemic inflammation** (SIRS/sepsis) → cytokine storm, microvascular dysfunction, blood–nerve and blood–muscle barrier injury → axonal degeneration and muscle fiber damage. [^1_2][^1_7]
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction and energy failure** in nerve and muscle; oxidative stress. [^1_4]
- **Acquired sodium channelopathy**: reduced membrane excitability in nerves and muscle, contributing to inexcitability and weakness. [^1_4]
- **CIM-specific**: selective myosin loss, muscle atrophy, necrosis; direct steroid and NMBA effects on muscle membrane and protein turnover. [^1_1][^1_6]
- **CIP-specific**: distal axonal degeneration of motor and sensory fibers without primary demyelination. [^1_1][^1_3]


## Clinical Presentation

- **Symmetrical flaccid weakness** of limbs (proximal and distal), often more prominent in lower limbs. [^1_5][^1_3]
- **Respiratory muscle weakness**: difficulty weaning, recurrent extubation failure, hypercapnic respiratory failure after other causes excluded. [^1_1][^1_12]
- **Reduced or absent deep tendon reflexes**; facial and ocular muscles usually spared. [^1_5][^1_3]
- **Sensory deficits** may be present but are hard to assess in sedated patients; CIP tends to have more sensory involvement than CIM. [^1_1][^1_3]
- Onset: typically **days to weeks** after ICU admission during or after sepsis/MOF. [^1_5][^1_2]


## Diagnostic Approach

### Step 1: Clinical suspicion

Suspect ICUAW/CIP-CIM in any patient with:

- New limb weakness after sepsis/MOF/ARDS, or
- Unexplained difficulty weaning from ventilator after cardiorespiratory causes are excluded. [^1_1][^1_12][^1_3]


### Step 2: Exclude non-neuromuscular causes of weakness/weaning failure

- Cardiac: LV failure, arrhythmias.
- Pulmonary: pneumonia, pneumothorax, severe bronchospasm, PE.
- Metabolic: severe acid-base, electrolyte abnormalities.
- Sedation/residual paralysis: ensure adequate wake-up, check for residual NMBA effect. [^1_1][^1_3]


### Step 3: Bedside clinical assessment

- **MRC Sum Score** (in conscious, cooperative patients):
    - Grade 0–5 power in 6 muscle groups bilaterally (shoulder abduction, elbow flexion, wrist extension, hip flexion, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion).
    - Maximum score = 60; **ICUAW defined as MRC sum score <48**. [^1_3]
- Pattern: symmetric, flaccid, reduced reflexes, no cranial nerve involvement helps distinguish from many mimics. [^1_5][^1_3]


### Step 4: Laboratory tests (supportive, not definitive)

- **CK**: may be normal or mildly elevated (more in CIM, especially with necrosis). [^1_3]
- Electrolytes, phosphate, magnesium, calcium, LFTs, renal function, glucose/HbA1c.
- Rule out metabolic/toxic causes: thyroid function, B12, porphyria if indicated. [^1_11][^1_13]


### Step 5: Electrophysiology (key to diagnosis)

Performed by neurology/physiatry; differentiates CIP vs CIM vs other neuromuscular disorders.

#### CIP (typical EMG/NCS findings)

- **Nerve conduction studies**:
    - Reduced CMAP and SNAP amplitudes (axonal loss).
    - Relatively preserved conduction velocities and distal latencies (not primarily demyelinating).
- **EMG**:
    - Axonal motor and sensory polyneuropathy pattern.
    - Fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves in subacute phase.
- **Repetitive nerve stimulation**: no decrement (helps exclude NMJ disorders). [^1_1][^1_3]


#### CIM (typical EMG/NCS findings)

- **Nerve conduction studies**:
    - **Preserved SNAP amplitudes** (>80% of lower limit of normal).
    - **Reduced CMAP amplitudes** (<80% of lower limit) in ≥2 nerves, without conduction block.
- **EMG**:
    - Short-duration, low-amplitude motor unit potentials with early/normal recruitment.
    - May show fibrillation potentials.
- **Direct muscle stimulation** (in uncooperative patients):
    - Reduced muscle membrane excitability / prolonged CMAP duration. [^1_1][^1_6][^1_3]


#### CIPNM (overlap)

- Features of both: reduced CMAP and SNAP, plus myopathic EMG changes. [^1_1][^1_2]


### Step 6: Muscle biopsy (when diagnosis uncertain or for research)

Indications: atypical presentation, need to exclude other myopathies, or when EMG inconclusive. [^1_1][^1_6]

#### CIM histopathology

- Muscle fiber necrosis, myosin loss, atrophy.
- Inflammatory infiltrates usually minimal compared with inflammatory myopathies. [^1_1][^1_6]


## Diagnostic Criteria (Simplified for Exams)

### Definite CIP

1. Critically ill patient with new limb weakness or weaning difficulty after excluding cardiac/pulmonary causes.
2. EMG/NCS: **axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy**.
3. No decrement on repetitive nerve stimulation. [^1_1][^1_3]

### Definite CIM

1. Critically ill patient with new limb weakness or weaning difficulty after excluding cardiac/pulmonary causes.
2. NCS: **CMAP <80%** of lower limit in ≥2 nerves; **SNAP >80%** of lower limit.
3. EMG: short-duration, low-amplitude MUPs with early/normal recruitment; or direct muscle stimulation showing reduced excitability.
4. No decrement on repetitive nerve stimulation.
5. Muscle biopsy consistent with primary myopathy (for definite CIM in some criteria). [^1_1][^1_6][^1_3]

## Differential Diagnosis

Key is to distinguish CIP/CIM from other acute causes of paralysis/respiratory failure in ICU. [^1_5][^1_11][^1_13][^1_10]


| Condition | Clues favoring alternative diagnosis |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)** | Often antecedent infection (GI/respiratory) before ICU; CSF albuminocytologic dissociation; NCS may show demyelination (AIDP) or axonal variants; can have cranial nerve involvement. [^1_5][^1_3] |
| **Myasthenic crisis** | Fluctuating weakness, ocular/bulbar involvement, fatigability; positive AChR/MuSK antibodies; decrement on repetitive nerve stimulation; response to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. [^1_5][^1_10] |
| **Prolonged neuromuscular blockade** | Recent or ongoing NMBA use; train-of-four monitoring shows persistent block; improvement with time and/or reversal agents; EMG shows NMJ transmission defect. [^1_5][^1_10] |
| **Metabolic myopathies** (hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, etc.) | Clear electrolyte abnormalities; rapid improvement with correction; no primary axonal neuropathy on EMG. [^1_11] |
| **Drug-induced myopathies** (statins, antiretrovirals, chloroquine) | Temporal relation to drug; CK often elevated; EMG/biopsy show myopathy but not necessarily CIP pattern. [^1_11] |
| **Spinal cord lesions** (ischemia, compression) | Sensory level, sphincter disturbance, asymmetric or paraplegic pattern; MRI spine diagnostic. [^1_13] |
| **Motor neuron disease** | Chronic progressive course, UMN + LMN signs, not acute onset in ICU. [^1_11][^1_13] |
| **Porphyria, toxins** | Abdominal pain, psychiatric symptoms, specific exposures; appropriate labs. [^1_1][^1_13] |

## Management

No disease-specific pharmacologic therapy; management is largely preventive and supportive. [^1_2][^1_6][^1_14][^1_4]

### 1. Prevention and Risk-Factor Modification

- **Sepsis control**: early recognition, appropriate antibiotics, source control, hemodynamic optimization. [^1_2][^1_4]
- **Glycemic control**: avoid severe hyperglycemia; consider protocols targeting moderate control (balance with hypoglycemia risk). [^1_7][^1_4]
- **Minimize steroids and NMBAs**:
    - Use lowest effective dose and shortest duration.
    - Avoid continuous NMBA infusions unless absolutely necessary; monitor with train-of-four. [^1_7][^1_10][^1_9]
- **Early mobilization and rehabilitation**:
    - Passive/active range-of-motion exercises as soon as hemodynamically stable.
    - Progress to sitting, standing, walking with PT/OT support. [^1_2][^1_4]
- **Nutritional support**: adequate protein and calories to limit muscle catabolism. [^1_2]
- **Avoid unnecessary neurotoxic drugs**; review medication list for statins, aminoglycosides, etc., in high-risk patients. [^1_5][^1_11]


### 2. Supportive ICU Care

- **Ventilator management**:
    - Lung-protective ventilation.
    - Daily sedation interruption and spontaneous breathing trials where safe to reduce ventilation duration. [^1_2][^1_4]
- **Hemodynamic optimization**: maintain perfusion to nerves and muscles; avoid prolonged shock. [^1_2]
- **Electrolyte correction**: maintain K+, Mg2+, PO43−, Ca2+ in normal range. [^1_11]
- **Pressure injury prevention**: careful positioning, padding, turning schedules to avoid additional nerve compression. [^1_2]


### 3. Rehabilitation and Recovery

- **Early PT/OT in ICU**: passive/active exercises, electrical muscle stimulation (where available), positioning. [^1_2][^1_4]
- **Post-ICU rehabilitation**: structured programs for strength, endurance, balance, gait training.
- **Multidisciplinary follow-up**: ICU clinic, neurology, physiatry, respiratory therapy.
- Prognosis: many patients improve over weeks to months, but a subset has persistent weakness and disability. [^1_2][^1_4]


### 4. Pharmacologic Therapy

- **No proven disease-modifying drug** for CIP/CIM at present. [^1_6][^1_14]
- Treat underlying conditions (sepsis, organ failure) aggressively.
- Avoid empiric steroids or IVIG unless an alternative diagnosis (e.g., GBS, myasthenia, inflammatory myopathy) is strongly suspected. [^1_14][^1_10]


## Prognosis

- Mortality is higher in patients with CIP/CIM, partly reflecting severity of underlying illness. [^1_1][^1_2]
- Survivors often have:
    - Prolonged weaning and ICU stay.
    - Long-term muscle weakness, reduced exercise capacity, impaired QoL. [^1_2][^1_4]
- Recovery can continue for months; early rehabilitation improves functional outcomes. [^1_2][^1_4]


## Landmark Trials and Guidelines (Exam-Relevant Points)

- **Intensive insulin therapy** (Leuven studies): associated with reduced incidence of CIP in surgical ICU patients, supporting a role for hyperglycemia in pathogenesis. [^1_7]
- Consensus guidelines (e.g., from neurocritical care and neuromuscular societies) emphasize:
    - Clinical suspicion in sepsis/MOF/ARDS with weaning failure.
    - Use of MRC score, EMG/NCS, and selective biopsy.
    - Prevention via sepsis control, steroid/NMBA minimization, early mobilization. [^1_2][^1_6][^1_3]


## Key Clinical Pearls (High-Yield for Exams)

- Think **CIP/CIM** in any septic/MOF/ARDS patient who **cannot be weaned** and has **flaccid symmetric limb weakness** with **reduced reflexes**. [^1_1][^1_5][^1_3]
- **CIP**: axonal sensorimotor neuropathy (low CMAP + low SNAP). [^1_1][^1_3]
- **CIM**: myopathy with **preserved SNAP**, **low CMAP**, myosin loss; strongly linked to **steroids + NMBAs**. [^1_1][^1_6][^1_9]
- **MRC sum score <48** = ICU-acquired weakness. [^1_3]
- **No specific drug treatment**; focus on **prevention**, **sepsis control**, **glycemic control**, **minimizing steroids/NMBAs**, and **early rehab**. [^1_2][^1_14][^1_4]
- Always exclude **GBS, myasthenic crisis, prolonged NMBA effect, metabolic causes, and spinal cord lesions** before labeling as CIP/CIM. [^1_5][^1_11][^1_13][^1_10]
