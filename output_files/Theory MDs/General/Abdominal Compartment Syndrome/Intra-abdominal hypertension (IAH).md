## Intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH)
 
## Definition and Overview

**Intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH)** is defined as a sustained or repeated pathological elevation of intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) **≥12 mmHg**. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3][^1_4] Normal IAP in critically ill adults is approximately **5–7 mmHg**, and values up to 12 mmHg may be seen in obesity or pregnancy without necessarily indicating pathology. [^1_5][^1_4][^1_6]

**Abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS)** represents the progression to organ dysfunction: sustained IAP **>20 mmHg** associated with **new-onset organ failure**. [^1_5][^1_7][^1_3][^1_4]

***

## Classification and Grading

The World Society of the Abdominal Compartment Syndrome (WSACS) grading system: [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3][^1_4][^1_8][^1_6]


| Grade | IAP (mmHg) |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Normal** | 5–7 (up to 12 in some contexts) |
| **Grade I** | 12–15 |
| **Grade II** | 16–20 |
| **Grade III** | 21–25 |
| **Grade IV** | >25 |

**Key distinction:** IAH is a pressure diagnosis; ACS requires **pressure + organ dysfunction**. [^1_5][^1_3][^1_4]

***

## Etiology and Risk Factors

IAH/ACS arises from **increased intra-abdominal content**, **reduced abdominal wall compliance**, or **capillary leak with visceral edema**. [^1_1][^1_9][^1_6]

### Common Causes

- **Abdominal:** trauma, hemorrhage, pancreatitis, peritonitis, bowel obstruction/ileus, intra-abdominal infection/abscess, ascites, abdominal packing, tight closure after laparotomy. [^1_1][^1_10][^1_3]
- **Systemic/critical illness:** sepsis, massive fluid resuscitation, burns, coagulopathy, acidosis, hypothermia, mechanical ventilation with high PEEP. [^1_1][^1_3][^1_9]
- **Patient factors:** obesity, pregnancy, ascites, chronic liver disease. [^1_5][^1_4]


### High-Risk ICU Scenarios

- Severe sepsis/septic shock with large-volume resuscitation
- Major trauma with abdominal injury or retroperitoneal hemorrhage
- Post-operative abdominal surgery, especially with planned temporary closure
- Acute pancreatitis with significant third-spacing
- Massive transfusion protocols

***

## Pathophysiology

Elevated IAP exerts **direct mechanical compression** and **indirect hemodynamic effects** on multiple organ systems: [^1_1][^1_5][^1_8][^1_9][^1_6]

### Cardiovascular

- **Reduced venous return** from IVC compression → decreased preload → reduced cardiac output. [^1_3][^1_8]
- Increased intrathoracic pressure transmitted upward → elevated CVP and PAOP (may **falsely suggest volume overload**). [^1_8]
- Compensatory tachycardia and vasoconstriction; eventual hypotension if uncompensated. [^1_8]


### Respiratory

- Diaphragmatic cephalad displacement → reduced lung compliance, increased peak/plateau pressures. [^1_3][^1_6]
- Basal atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, hypercapnia, hypoxemia. [^1_3][^1_6]
- Difficulty weaning from ventilation; may require higher PEEP but risk worsening IAP. [^1_3]


### Renal

- Direct renal vein/parenchymal compression + reduced renal perfusion pressure → oliguria/anuria. [^1_3][^1_6]
- Activation of RAAS and ADH → sodium and water retention, worsening edema. [^1_6]


### Gastrointestinal/Hepatic

- Splanchnic hypoperfusion → gut ischemia, bacterial translocation, ileus. [^1_9][^1_6]
- Portal vein compression → hepatic dysfunction, lactic acidosis. [^1_6]


### CNS

- Increased IAP → reduced cerebral perfusion pressure (especially in traumatic brain injury). [^1_6]
- Elevated intrathoracic pressure impedes venous drainage from brain. [^1_6]


### Abdominal Perfusion Pressure (APP)

A critical concept in IAH management:

$$
\text{APP} = \text{MAP} - \text{IAP}
$$

- **Target APP ≥60 mmHg** is associated with improved survival. [^1_7][^1_8]
- APP integrates perfusion pressure relative to abdominal pressure; more physiologically relevant than MAP alone in IAH. [^1_8]

***

## Clinical Features

IAH is often **clinically silent** until organ dysfunction develops. [^1_1][^1_9]

### Bedside Clues

- **Abdominal distension**, tense abdomen, increased girth
- Rising ventilator pressures, worsening oxygenation
- Oliguria despite adequate fluid resuscitation
- Unexplained hypotension or escalating vasopressor requirement
- Increasing lactate, metabolic acidosis
- Difficulty with enteral feeding (high gastric residuals)

**Caution:** In sedated/paralyzed patients, abdominal exam may be unreliable—**IAP measurement is essential** in at-risk patients. [^1_7][^1_3]

***

## Diagnosis and Measurement

### Gold Standard: Bladder Pressure Measurement

- **Technique:** Foley catheter with ≤25 mL sterile saline in bladder, patient supine, transducer zeroed at mid-axillary line, end-expiratory measurement. [^1_7][^1_3]
- **Frequency:** Every 4–6 hours in patients with ≥2 risk factors for IAH/ACS. [^1_7]


### Diagnostic Criteria

- **IAH:** Sustained IAP ≥12 mmHg on repeated measurements. [^1_1][^1_3][^1_4]
- **ACS:** Sustained IAP >20 mmHg + new organ dysfunction (e.g., oliguria, hypoxemia, hypotension, rising lactate). [^1_5][^1_7][^1_3]


### Additional Monitoring

- **Abdominal perfusion pressure (APP):** MAP − IAP; target ≥60 mmHg. [^1_7][^1_8]
- Daily weight, cumulative fluid balance
- Urine output, lactate, base deficit
- Gastric residual volumes
- Advanced hemodynamic monitoring in severe IAH (e.g., echocardiography, cardiac output monitoring). [^1_7]

***

## Differential Diagnosis

Consider other causes of organ dysfunction in critically ill:

- **Cardiogenic shock** (elevated CVP/PAOP from primary cardiac failure)
- **Tension pneumothorax** (respiratory compromise, hypotension)
- **Cardiac tamponade** (obstructive shock physiology)
- **Severe ARDS** (high pressures, hypoxemia without abdominal cause)
- **Primary renal failure** (intrinsic AKI vs. compressive)
- **Sepsis-induced vasodilation** (low SVR, warm shock)

**Key differentiator:** Elevated IAP on bladder measurement confirms IAH/ACS. [^1_3]

***

## Management

Management follows a **stepwise approach**: medical therapies first, escalating to surgical decompression if refractory. [^1_10][^1_7][^1_3]

### General Principles

- **Early recognition** in at-risk patients with serial IAP monitoring. [^1_7]
- **Treat underlying cause** (e.g., control sepsis, drain ascites, relieve obstruction). [^1_10][^1_7]
- **Optimize APP** by maintaining MAP and reducing IAP. [^1_8]


### Medical/Non-Surgical Measures

1. **Evacuate intraluminal contents**
    - Nasogastric/rectal tubes for decompression
    - Prokinetics, enemas for ileus/constipation
    - Consider colonoscopic decompression in select cases. [^1_10]
2. **Evacuate intra-abdominal fluid**
    - Percutaneous drainage of ascites, abscess, hemoperitoneum
    - Image-guided catheter placement. [^1_10][^1_7]
3. **Optimize fluid balance**
    - Avoid excessive crystalloid; consider albumin in select cases
    - Diuretics once hemodynamically stable
    - Negative fluid balance strategies in post-resuscitation phase. [^1_10][^1_7]
4. **Improve abdominal wall compliance**
    - Adequate analgesia and sedation
    - Neuromuscular blockade in severe cases (reduces muscle tone)
    - Avoid tight abdominal binders or dressings. [^1_10][^1_7]
5. **Optimize positioning**
    - Avoid Trendelenburg; consider reverse Trendelenburg or semi-recumbent if tolerated. [^1_10]
6. **Hemodynamic support**
    - Vasopressors (norepinephrine) to maintain MAP and APP ≥60 mmHg. [^1_8]
    - Avoid over-resuscitation with fluids once IAH established. [^1_7][^1_8]
7. **Respiratory management**
    - Lung-protective ventilation; accept higher plateau pressures if necessary
    - Prone positioning may be challenging; monitor IAP closely. [^1_3]

### Surgical Management: Decompressive Laparotomy

**Indications:** [^1_7][^1_3]

- ACS with organ dysfunction refractory to medical therapy
- IAP >20–25 mmHg with worsening physiology despite maximal medical management
- Clinical deterioration (rising lactate, oliguria, escalating vasopressors)

**Procedure:**

- Midline laparotomy to release pressure
- Temporary abdominal closure (vacuum-assisted, Bogota bag, Wittmann patch)
- Plan for staged closure once edema resolves

**Risks:**

- Enteric fistula, infection, evisceration, loss of domain, hernia
- High mortality if delayed; timing is critical. [^1_3]

***

## Complications

- **Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS)** [^1_5][^1_9]
- **Acute kidney injury** requiring RRT [^1_3][^1_6]
- **Respiratory failure** requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation [^1_3]
- **Splanchnic ischemia**, bacterial translocation, sepsis [^1_9][^1_6]
- **Abdominal wall complications** post-decompression (hernia, fistula) [^1_3]
- **Increased mortality** with delayed recognition/treatment [^1_1][^1_9]

***

## Prognosis

- IAH prevalence in ICU: **30–50%**; ACS: **5–15%**. [^1_1][^1_3]
- Mortality increases with **higher IAP grades** and **delayed decompression**. [^1_1][^1_3][^1_9]
- Early recognition and stepwise management improve outcomes. [^1_7][^1_9]

***

## Topic Pearls

- **IAH = IAP ≥12 mmHg; ACS = IAP >20 mmHg + organ failure.** [^1_1][^1_3]
- **Bladder pressure** is the gold standard for IAP measurement; measure every 4–6 h in at-risk ICU patients. [^1_7][^1_3]
- **APP = MAP − IAP**; target **≥60 mmHg** for adequate abdominal organ perfusion. [^1_7][^1_8]
- IAH affects **all organ systems**: cardiovascular (↓ preload), respiratory (↓ compliance), renal (oliguria), GI (ischemia). [^1_3][^1_8][^1_6]
- **Medical management first**: decompress lumen, drain fluid, optimize fluids, improve compliance, vasopressors for APP. [^1_10][^1_7]
- **Decompressive laparotomy** is last resort for refractory ACS with organ dysfunction. [^1_7][^1_3]
- **Think IAH** in any ICU patient with unexplained oliguria, rising ventilator pressures, or hypotension despite resuscitation. [^1_1][^1_9]
