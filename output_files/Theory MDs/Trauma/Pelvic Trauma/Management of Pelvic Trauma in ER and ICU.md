
## Management of Pelvic Trauma in ER and ICU

Pelvic trauma represents one of the most challenging scenarios in emergency and critical care medicine, with mortality rates reaching 50% in hemodynamically unstable patients due to exsanguination. [^1_1][^1_2] The cornerstone of management is **early recognition, rapid hemorrhage control, and coordinated multidisciplinary care** involving trauma surgery, orthopedics, interventional radiology, and critical care teams. [^1_3][^1_4]

***

## 1. Classification Systems

### Young-Burgess Classification (Mechanism-Based) ★★

| Type | Mechanism | Stability | Key Features |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **APC I** | Anterior-Posterior Compression | Stable | Symphysis diastasis <2.5 cm; SI ligaments intact |
| **APC II** | AP Compression | Rotationally unstable | Symphysis >2.5 cm; anterior SI ligaments torn; posterior SI intact ("open book") |
| **APC III** | AP Compression | Completely unstable | Complete SI ligament disruption; symphysis + SI joint diastasis |
| **LC I** | Lateral Compression | Stable | Ipsilateral pubic rami fractures; sacral compression |
| **LC II** | Lateral Compression | Rotationally unstable | Ipsilateral rami + posterior iliac wing fracture (crescent fracture) |
| **LC III** | Lateral Compression | Completely unstable | Ipsilateral LC II + contralateral APC II/III ("windswept pelvis") |
| **VS** | Vertical Shear | Completely unstable | Vertical displacement; complete ligamentous disruption |

### Tile Classification (Stability-Based)

- **Type A**: Stable (no ring disruption) — iliac wing, avulsion fractures
- **Type B**: Rotationally unstable, vertically stable — APC I/II, LC I/II
- **Type C**: Rotationally AND vertically unstable — APC III, LC III, VS [^1_5][^1_6][^1_7]

***

## 2. Initial Assessment and Resuscitation

### Primary Survey (ATLS Protocol) ★

**Immediate actions on arrival:**

1. **Airway with cervical spine protection** — standard ATLS approach
2. **Breathing** — high-flow oxygen (15 L/min) until stable, then titrate to SpO₂ 94-98% [^1_8]
3. **Circulation** — two large-bore IVs (14-16G); aggressive fluid resuscitation followed by blood products if hypotensive [^1_1][^1_7]
4. **Disability** — GCS assessment
5. **Exposure** — full examination while maintaining warmth

### Hemorrhage Recognition

**Clinical indicators of significant pelvic hemorrhage:**

- Hemodynamic instability (SBP <90 mmHg, HR >110 bpm)
- Metabolic acidosis (base deficit >6, lactate >4 mmol/L)
- Coagulopathy on initial labs
- Transfusion requirement >2 units PRBC in first hour
- Pelvic instability on examination (crepitus, widening, leg length discrepancy)

[^1_3][^1_9][^1_10]

***

## 3. Pelvic Stabilization

### Pelvic Binder Application ★★

**Indications:**

- Any suspected pelvic ring disruption (high-energy mechanism + pelvic pain/instability)
- Hemodynamically stable OR unstable patients
- Apply BEFORE intubation (neuromuscular blockade may allow pelvic volume expansion) [^1_11]

**Technique:**

1. Patient supine
2. Adduct and internally rotate lower extremities (knees, ankles, toes together) [^1_9][^1_12]
3. Log-roll patient to slide binder underneath
4. **Position over greater trochanters** (NOT iliac crests) — critical error to avoid [^1_13][^1_12]
5. Tighten to 180 Newtons (equivalent to lifting 18 kg) [^1_13]
6. Secure and document time of application
7. Perform neurovascular assessment post-application [^1_13][^1_14]

**Duration:**

- Reassess within 6 hours of application
- Remove only when:
    - Other life threats addressed
    - Adequate IV access obtained
    - Patient euvolemic or blood products available
    - Imaging confirms no major injury OR advanced imaging not concerning for extensive blood loss
    - Surgical services present and prepared [^1_14]

[^1_11][^1_9][^1_13][^1_12][^1_14]

## 4. Diagnostic Algorithm

### Hemodynamically UNSTABLE Patient

**Immediate bedside evaluation:**

1. **AP Pelvis X-ray** — if normal, rules out pelvic fracture as cause of instability [^1_11][^1_9]
2. **E-FAST** — assess for hemothorax, pneumothorax, intraperitoneal free fluid
3. **DPA (Diagnostic Peritoneal Aspiration)** — if E-FAST negative, confirm absence of intraperitoneal blood [^1_11][^1_9]

**Decision pathway:**

- **E-FAST positive OR DPA positive** → Emergency laparotomy with preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP) ± external fixation [^1_11][^1_9]
- **E-FAST negative AND DPA negative** → Isolated pelvic hemorrhage suspected:
    - Pelvic binder in place
    - Activate massive transfusion protocol (MTP)
    - Consider TXA 1g IV if within 3 hours of injury [^1_9]
    - **REBOA** (Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta) for patients in extremis [^1_1][^1_9]
    - Proceed to **angiography/embolization** OR **preperitoneal packing** based on institutional capability [^1_1][^1_2][^1_15]


### Hemodynamically STABLE Patient

1. **AP Pelvis X-ray** — initial screening
2. **CT abdomen-pelvis with IV contrast** (multiphasic) — gold standard for:
    - Fracture pattern characterization
    - Contrast extravasation ("blush") indicating arterial bleeding
    - Associated abdominal/retroperitoneal injuries
    - CT cystography if bladder injury suspected [^1_11][^1_9][^1_4]

**CT findings mandating angiography:**

- Arterial contrast extravasation (blush)
- Pelvic hematoma volume >500 mL
- Major fracture patterns (APC II/III, LC II/III, VS)
- Age ≥60 years with major fracture [^1_9][^1_16]

[^1_11][^1_9][^1_4][^1_16][^1_10]

## 5. Hemorrhage Control Strategies

### Preperitoneal Pelvic Packing (PPP) ★

**Indications:**

- Hemodynamically unstable with isolated pelvic hemorrhage
- When angiography not immediately available
- As adjunct to angiography in massive bleeding

**Technique:**

- Suprapubic incision
- Manual dissection into preperitoneal space
- Pack with laparotomy sponges (typically 3-4 packs)
- Provides direct compression of bleeding venous plexus and fracture surfaces

**Efficacy:**

- Reduces transfusion requirements
- Can be combined with external fixation or C-clamp for posterior ring stabilization


### Angiography with Embolization (AE) ★★

**Indications:**

- Hemodynamically unstable with suspected pelvic arterial bleeding (after non-pelvic sources excluded)
- Arterial contrast extravasation on CT regardless of hemodynamic status
- Ongoing bleeding after initial resuscitation
- Elderly patients (≥60 years) with major fracture patterns
- Pelvic hematoma >500 mL [^1_9][^1_17][^1_16][^1_10]

**Technical considerations:**

- **Selective embolization** preferred over non-selective (lower complication rate) [^1_9]
- Targets internal iliac artery branches (superior gluteal, obturator, internal pudendal)
- Technical success: 90-100%
- Clinical success (improved vitals, decreased transfusion): 84-100% [^1_17][^1_10]
- Repeat angiography required in 4.6-24.3% of patients (persistent/recurrent bleeding) [^1_10]

**Limitations:**

- Controls only **arterial** hemorrhage
- Most pelvic bleeding is **venous** (fracture edges, presacral plexus, iliolumbar vein) — requires PPP or external fixation [^1_17][^1_10]


### Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta (REBOA)

**Indications:**

- Patients in extremis from pelvic hemorrhage
- Bridge to OR, angiography, or definitive hemorrhage control
- Zone 3 placement (below renal arteries, above aortic bifurcation)

**Considerations:**

- Temporary measure (max 60 minutes recommended)
- Requires experienced operator
- Risk of distal ischemia, reperfusion injury, aortic complications


### External Fixation

**Indications:**

- Rotationally unstable fractures (APC II, LC II)
- As damage control before definitive internal fixation
- Combined with PPP for posterior ring instability

**Devices:**

- C-clamp (posterior ring compression)
- Anterior external fixator frames

[^1_3][^1_18][^1_2][^1_7]

## 6. ICU Management

### Admission Criteria ★

**SICU/ICU admission indicated for:**

- Hemodynamic instability, metabolic acidosis, or coagulopathy
- Requirement for IR or surgical intervention for hemorrhage control
- Unstable pelvic fractures: LC II/III, APC II/III, VS
- Associated major injuries (TBI, thoracic, abdominal, long bone)
- Massive transfusion requirement (>4 units PRBC in first 24 hours)
- Need for ongoing vasopressor support or mechanical ventilation


### Hemodynamic Monitoring and Resuscitation

**Goals:**

- SBP 90-100 mmHg (permissive hypotension if no TBI)
- MAP ≥65 mmHg
- Urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/hr
- Lactate clearance (>10% per 2 hours)
- Base deficit normalization
- Hb 7-9 g/dL (restrictive strategy unless ongoing hemorrhage)

**Massive Transfusion Protocol:**

- Balanced resuscitation (PRBC:FFP:Platelets in 1:1:1 or 2:1:1 ratio)
- Early TXA 1g IV within 3 hours of injury (CRASH-2 trial) [^1_9]
- Calcium replacement (citrate toxicity from blood products)
- Temperature maintenance (>36°C)
- TEG/ROTEM-guided transfusion if available [^1_9]


### Pain Management

- Multimodal analgesia: opioids + acetaminophen + regional techniques
- Consider femoral nerve blocks or fascia iliaca blocks for anterior ring fractures
- Avoid excessive sedation that masks neurological deterioration
- Epidural analgesia for stable patients post-fixation


### VTE Prophylaxis ★

**Timing:**

- Initiate within 24 hours once hemorrhage controlled or hemodynamic stability achieved [^1_9]
- Mechanical prophylaxis (SCDs) immediately if chemoprophylaxis contraindicated

**Agents:**

- LMWH (enoxaparin 30 mg SC BID) — preferred
- Low-dose unfractionated heparin (5000 units SC TID) — alternative
- Continue until patient ambulatory or transitioned to therapeutic anticoagulation


### Infection Prevention

**Open pelvic fractures:**

- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., cefazolin + gentamicin + metronidazole, or carbapenem)
- Meticulous wound debridement
- Fecal diversion (colostomy) for rectal/perineal injuries
- Urinary diversion for urethral/bladder injuries
- Monitor for pelvic abscess, osteomyelitis, sepsis

[^1_2][^1_19]

## 7. Complications

### Early Complications (<72 hours)

| Complication | Risk Factors | Prevention/Management |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Hemorrhagic shock** | Unstable fracture patterns, delayed stabilization | Early binder, PPP, AE, MTP |
| **Coagulopathy** | Massive transfusion, hypothermia, acidosis | Balanced resuscitation, warming, calcium |
| **Abdominal compartment syndrome** | Massive transfusion, retroperitoneal hematoma | Monitor bladder pressure, decompressive laparotomy if needed |
| **DVT/PE** | Immobility, vascular injury | Early chemoprophylaxis, SCDs |
| **Compartment syndrome (lower limb)** | Associated long bone fractures, reperfusion | Serial neurovascular exams, fasciotomy |
| **Sepsis** | Open fractures, genitourinary/rectal injuries | Antibiotics, debridement, source control |

### Late Complications

- **Nonunion/malunion** — requires revision fixation
- **Chronic pelvic pain** — neuropathic pain management, nerve blocks
- **Sexual dysfunction** — nerve injury (pudendal, cavernous nerves)
- **Urinary incontinence/stricture** — urology follow-up
- **Heterotopic ossification** — indomethacin prophylaxis post-op
- **Gait disturbance/limb length discrepancy** — physiotherapy, orthotics

[^1_1]

***

## 8. Definitive Orthopedic Management

### Timing

- **Damage control external fixation** — within first 24 hours for unstable patients
- **Definitive internal fixation** — typically 3-7 days after hemodynamic stabilization, once soft tissue swelling resolved
- Delayed fixation (>2 weeks) associated with increased complications


### Surgical Approaches

- **Anterior ring**: ORIF with plates/screws via Pfannenstiel or ilioinguinal approach
- **Posterior ring**:
    - SI joint screws (percutaneous)
    - Posterior plating
    - Triangular osteosynthesis for vertical instability

[^1_4][^1_6]

***

## 9. Prognosis and Mortality

**Mortality rates:**

- Overall: 5-15%
- Hemodynamically stable: <5%
- Hemodynamically unstable: 30-50%
- With associated TBI or multi-system trauma: up to 60%

**Predictors of mortality:**

- Age >60 years
- ISS >25
- Base deficit >10
- Transfusion requirement >10 units PRBC in 24 hours
- Open pelvic fracture
- Associated intracranial hemorrhage

[^1_1][^1_2][^1_15]

***

## Topic Pearls

- **Pelvic binder over greater trochanters** (not iliac crests) — most common application error [^1_13][^1_12]
- **E-FAST + DPA** in unstable patients determines need for laparotomy vs. pelvic-specific hemorrhage control [^1_11][^1_9]
- **Angiography controls arterial bleeding only**; venous bleeding requires PPP or external fixation [^1_17][^1_10]
- **CT "blush" = angiography** regardless of hemodynamic status [^1_11][^1_16]
- **VTE prophylaxis within 24 hours** once bleeding controlled — critical for preventing PE [^1_9]
- **Open pelvic fractures** require broad-spectrum antibiotics, debridement, and often fecal/urinary diversion [^1_2][^1_19]
- **Mortality 30-50%** in hemodynamically unstable patients — early hemorrhage control is paramount [^1_1][^1_2]
