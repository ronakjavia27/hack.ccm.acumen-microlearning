
## Trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC)

**Trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC)** is a multifactorial, global failure of hemostasis attributable to traumatic injury and hemorrhagic shock, characterized by abnormal coagulation processes that result in both uncontrolled bleeding and, paradoxically, a later hypercoagulable state predisposing to venous thromboembolism and multiple organ failure. [^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

TIC is distinct from **acute traumatic coagulopathy (ATC)**, which refers specifically to the early endogenous coagulopathy present on hospital admission before significant resuscitation, whereas TIC encompasses the broader spectrum including resuscitation-associated factors (hypothermia, acidosis, dilution). [^1_4][^1_5]

## Epidemiology and Clinical Significance

- **Incidence**: Affects approximately **25–35%** of severely injured trauma patients on hospital admission. [^1_2][^1_3][^1_6]
- **Mortality impact**: Patients with TIC have nearly **4-fold higher mortality** compared to those with comparable Injury Severity Scores (ISS) without coagulopathy. [^1_7][^1_6]
- **Timing**: Present in one-quarter to one-third of hemorrhaging trauma patients; severity correlates directly with injury severity and shock. [^1_2][^1_3]
- **Preventable deaths**: Most early preventable trauma deaths in civilian and military settings are due to uncontrolled hemorrhage, with TIC being a major contributor. [^1_2][^1_7]


## Pathophysiology

TIC represents a **maladaptive hemostatic and inflammatory response** to trauma, with phenotypes ranging from bleeding to prothrombotic states depending on injury pattern, shock severity, and individual response. [^1_8][^1_6]

### Core Pathophysiological Mechanisms

#### 1. **Endothelial Activation and Glycocalyx Damage**

- Tissue injury and shock synergistically provoke **systemic endothelial activation**. [^1_1]
- **Glycocalyx shedding** releases heparin-like substances contributing to anticoagulation.
- Endothelial damage exposes tissue factor and von Willebrand factor, triggering coagulation cascade activation. [^1_6]


#### 2. **Protein C Pathway Dysregulation**

- **Activated protein C (APC)** is upregulated in response to tissue injury and hypoperfusion. [^1_3][^1_9]
- APC exerts anticoagulant effects by inactivating factors Va and VIIIa.
- Simultaneously, APC inhibits plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), promoting **hyperfibrinolysis**. [^1_3]


#### 3. **Hyperfibrinolysis**

- Increased **tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)** release from endothelium. [^1_3][^1_9]
- Decreased PAI-1 levels (due to APC) reduce fibrinolysis inhibition.
- Results in accelerated clot breakdown and **fibrinogen depletion**. [^1_1][^1_5]
- **Fibrinolytic phenotype** is characteristic of TIC and distinguishes it from classic disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). [^1_5]


#### 4. **Coagulation Factor Consumption and Dysfunction**

- **Fibrinogen depletion**: Early and profound reduction due to consumption and fibrinolysis. [^1_1][^1_3]
- **Inadequate thrombin generation**: Despite initial activation, thrombin burst is impaired. [^1_1][^1_9]
- **Clotting factor consumption**: Factors II, V, VIII, and XIII are consumed in microvascular thrombosis. [^1_3]


#### 5. **Platelet Dysfunction**

- **Impaired platelet function** despite often preserved platelet counts. [^1_1][^1_3]
- Mechanisms include:
    - Shedding of platelet surface receptors (GP1b, GPVI)
    - Reduced aggregation response to ADP, collagen, and thrombin
    - Platelet exhaustion from widespread activation [^1_9]


#### 6. **Inflammatory Response**

- TIC is fundamentally a **global inflammatory state**. [^1_3]
- Tissue injury triggers systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).
- Inflammation and coagulation are bidirectionally linked (immunothrombosis).
- Cytokine release (IL-6, TNF-α) further activates endothelium and coagulation. [^1_2]


### Dynamic Evolution: From Hypocoagulability to Hypercoagulability

| Phase | Timing | Dominant Phenotype | Clinical Manifestation |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Early** | 0–6 hours post-trauma | **Hypocoagulable** with hyperfibrinolysis | Uncontrolled bleeding, hemorrhagic shock |
| **Intermediate** | 6–24 hours | Transition phase | Variable; ongoing bleeding or stabilization |
| **Late** | >24 hours | **Hypercoagulable** | Venous thromboembolism (VTE), multiple organ failure (MOF) |

[^1_10][^1_1][^1_6]

**Key concept**: Blunt trauma without shock tends toward **thrombotic phenotypes**, while severe trauma with hemorrhagic shock produces **bleeding phenotypes**. [^1_6]

## The Lethal Triad and Diamond

### Classic Lethal Triad

TIC is accentuated by the traditional "lethal triad" of trauma: [^1_1][^1_3]

1. **Coagulopathy** (TIC itself)
2. **Hypothermia** (<33°C significantly decreases platelet and coagulation factor activity) [^1_3]
3. **Metabolic acidosis** (impairs coagulation enzyme function and platelet aggregation)

### Expanded Lethal Diamond

Modern understanding adds a fourth component:
4. **Hypocalcemia** (ionized calcium <1.0 mmol/L impairs coagulation cascade at multiple steps)

## Diagnosis

TIC lacks universally accepted quantitative diagnostic criteria but can be identified through: [^1_3][^1_8]

### Conventional Coagulation Tests (CCTs)

- **Platelet count**: May be normal despite dysfunction [^1_3]
- **Prothrombin time (PT) / INR**: Often elevated but lags behind clinical coagulopathy
- **Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)**: Prolonged in severe cases
- **Fibrinogen (Clauss assay)**: Critical marker; levels <150–200 mg/dL associated with increased bleeding [^1_3]
- **D-dimer**: Markedly elevated reflecting fibrinolysis
- **Limitations**: CCTs have 30–60 minute turnaround time and do not assess fibrinolysis or platelet function adequately. [^1_3]


### Viscoelastic Hemostatic Assays (VHAs)

**Preferred for rapid, comprehensive assessment** at bedside: [^1_3]


| Assay | Parameters | Clinical Utility |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **TEG** (Thromboelastography) | R-time, K-time, α-angle, MA, LY30 | Assesses clot initiation, kinetics, strength, and lysis |
| **ROTEM** (Rotational Thromboelastometry) | CT, CFT, α-angle, MCF, LI30 | Similar to TEG; EXTEM, FIBTEM, APTEM assays |

**VHA advantages**:

- Results in 10–20 minutes
- Detects hyperfibrinolysis (LY30 >3% or LI30 <85%)
- Guides targeted transfusion (fibrinogen, platelets, antifibrinolytics) [^1_3]


### Clinical Scoring Systems

- **Trauma Induced Coagulopathy Clinical Score (TICCS)**: Incorporates clinical and laboratory variables for risk stratification. [^1_3]
- **ABC score** (Assessment of Blood Consumption): Predicts massive transfusion need.
- **TASH score** (Trauma-Associated Severe Hemorrhage): Another massive transfusion predictor.


## Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Classic DIC** | More gradual onset; associated with sepsis, malignancy, obstetric complications; less prominent fibrinolysis |
| **Liver disease coagulopathy** | Chronic elevation of INR; low fibrinogen; thrombocytopenia from hypersplenism; history of liver disease |
| **Anticoagulant therapy** | Warfarin (elevated INR), DOACs (prolonged aPTT/anti-Xa); medication history |
| **Hemophilia/von Willebrand disease** | Personal/family history; specific factor deficiencies; not acute post-trauma |
| **Dilutional coagulopathy** | Occurs after large-volume crystalloid/colloid resuscitation; later in timeline than ATC |
| **Hypothermia-induced** | Core temperature <33°C; corrects with warming |

## Investigations

### Immediate (Emergency Department/Resuscitation Bay)

- **Point-of-care VHA** (TEG/ROTEM) if available [^1_3]
- **CBC with platelet count**
- **PT/INR, aPTT**
- **Fibrinogen** (Clauss method; critical target >150–200 mg/dL) [^1_3]
- **Ionized calcium** (target >1.0–1.2 mmol/L)
- **Arterial blood gas**: pH, lactate, base deficit (shock severity)
- **Type and crossmatch**: Prepare for massive transfusion


### Additional/Specialized

- **D-dimer**: Marker of fibrinolysis
- **Thrombin time**: Prolonged with hypofibrinogenemia or heparin effect
- **Factor assays**: If specific factor deficiency suspected
- **Platelet function assays** (PFA-100, Multiplate): Research/specialized centers


## Management

Management priorities: **Stop bleeding, reverse shock, restore hemostasis**. [^1_1]

### 1. Hemorrhage Control (Definitive)

- **Surgical intervention**: Damage control surgery, packing, vascular control
- **Interventional radiology**: Angiographic embolization for pelvic, solid organ bleeding
- **Mechanical measures**: Pelvic binders, tourniquets, wound packing [^1_3]


### 2. Damage Control Resuscitation (DCR)

#### A. **Blood Product Resuscitation**

**Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)** activation criteria:

- Anticipated need for ≥10 units PRBC in 24 hours
- Physiologic triggers: SBP <90 mmHg, HR >120, positive FAST, penetrating trauma

**Balanced transfusion ratios**:

- **1:1:1** (PRBC:FFP:Platelets) or **1:1:2** targets [^1_3]
- **Whole blood** (low-titer O-positive whole blood) increasingly preferred in trauma centers
- **FFP**: Target INR <1.5; dose 15–30 mL/kg
- **Platelets**: Target >50,000/μL (or >100,000/μL in TBI); dose 1 apheresis unit or 4–6 pooled units
- **Cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen concentrate**: For fibrinogen <150–200 mg/dL [^1_3]


#### B. **Tranexamic Acid (TXA)**

- **CRASH-2 trial**: TXA 1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g infusion over 8 hours [^1_3]
- **Timing**: Administer within **3 hours** of injury (benefit lost if delayed)
- **Indication**: SBP <90 mmHg or HR >110 bpm, or anticipated massive transfusion
- **Mechanism**: Antifibrinolytic; inhibits plasminogen activation
- **Caution**: Avoid in isolated TBI without systemic trauma (CRASH-3 showed no benefit; possible harm)


#### C. **Calcium Replacement**

- **Ionized calcium target**: >1.0–1.2 mmol/L [^1_3]
- Citrate in blood products chelates calcium
- **Dosing**: 1–2 g calcium chloride (central) or 2–3 g calcium gluconate (peripheral) per 4–6 units PRBC


#### D. **Temperature Management**

- **Target**: Core temperature >35°C (ideally >36°C) [^1_3]
- **Measures**:
    - Warm IV fluids and blood products (fluid warmers)
    - Forced-air warming blankets
    - Increase ambient temperature
    - Minimize body exposure during surveys [^1_3]


#### E. **Acidosis Correction**

- **Primary strategy**: Restore perfusion (hemorrhage control, volume resuscitation)
- Avoid excessive crystalloids (worsens dilution and acidosis)
- **Sodium bicarbonate**: Only for severe acidemia (pH <7.1) with hemodynamic compromise


### 3. Avoid Crystalloid Resuscitation

- **Crystalloids worsen TIC** through:
    - Dilution of coagulation factors and platelets
    - Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis
    - Endothelial glycocalyx damage
- **Limit**: <1–1.5 L crystalloid in first 24 hours if possible [^1_7]
- **Preferred**: Blood products, balanced salt solutions in small volumes if needed


### 4. Targeted Factor Replacement

#### Fibrinogen

- **Target**: >150–200 mg/dL (some guidelines suggest >200 mg/dL in severe bleeding) [^1_3]
- **Cryoprecipitate**: 10 units raises fibrinogen by ~50–100 mg/dL
- **Fibrinogen concentrate**: 3–4 g IV (dosed by fibrinogen level and weight)


#### Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (PCC)

- **4-factor PCC**: Contains factors II, VII, IX, X
- **Investigational in trauma**: Early prehospital administration being studied [^1_3]
- **Consider**: If INR remains elevated despite FFP, or in anticoagulated patients


#### Recombinant Factor VIIa

- **Not routinely recommended**: Associated with increased thrombotic complications
- **Rescue therapy only**: After surgical control, adequate fibrinogen/platelets, and correction of pH/temperature/calcium


### 5. Monitoring and Escalation

#### Clinical Monitoring

- **Vital signs**: HR, BP, urine output (>0.5 mL/kg/hr)
- **Lactate clearance**: Target >10% decrease per 2 hours
- **Base deficit**: Normalization indicates improved perfusion
- **Bleeding assessment**: Ongoing transfusion requirements, surgical field bleeding


#### Laboratory Monitoring

- **VHA every 30–60 minutes** during active resuscitation (if available)
- **CCTs every 1–2 hours**: INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, platelets
- **Ionized calcium**: Every 30–60 minutes during MTP
- **ABG**: Every 30–60 minutes initially


#### Escalation Criteria

- Persistent coagulopathy despite balanced transfusion
- Fibrinogen refractory to cryoprecipitate
- Uncontrolled surgical bleeding
- Consider: Additional surgical exploration, IR embolization, PCC, fibrinogen concentrate


## Complications

### Early (0–72 hours)

- **Uncontrolled hemorrhage**: Leading cause of early preventable death [^1_2][^1_7]
- **Multiple organ failure (MOF)**: From ongoing hypoperfusion and inflammation
- **Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)**
- **Acute kidney injury (AKI)**


### Late (>72 hours)

- **Venous thromboembolism (VTE)**: DVT, PE from hypercoagulable phase [^1_1][^1_6]
- **Thrombotic microangiopathy**
- **Ischemic complications**: Limb, visceral organ ischemia
- **Transfusion-related complications**:
    - TRALI (Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury)
    - TACO (Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload)
    - Transfusion-transmitted infections (rare)
    - Alloimmunization


## Prognosis

- **Mortality**: 4-fold higher in TIC patients vs. non-coagulopathic trauma patients with similar ISS. [^1_6]
- **Predictors of poor outcome**:
    - Admission INR >1.5
    - Fibrinogen <150 mg/dL
    - Platelet count <100,000/μL
    - Hyperfibrinolysis on VHA (LY30 >3%)
    - Base deficit >6 mEq/L
    - Lactate >4 mmol/L
    - Hypothermia <34°C
    - pH <7.2
- **Survivors**: Risk of VTE persists for weeks; requires extended thromboprophylaxis


## Prevention

### Prehospital Phase

- **Early hemorrhage control**: Tourniquets, wound packing, pelvic binders [^1_3]
- **Limited crystalloid**: Permissive hypotension (SBP 80–90 mmHg) unless TBI
- **TXA administration**: Within 3 hours of injury [^1_3]
- **Blood product resuscitation**: Prehospital plasma/whole blood in some systems
- **Rapid transport**: Minimize time to definitive hemorrhage control [^1_7]


### Hospital Phase

- **Early MTP activation**: Don't wait for laboratory confirmation
- **Balanced transfusion**: 1:1:1 or whole blood from outset
- **Temperature management**: From arrival (warm room, fluids, blood) [^1_3]
- **Calcium monitoring/replacement**: Proactive, not reactive
- **Avoid excessive crystalloids**: Blood-first resuscitation [^1_7]
- **Early surgical/IR intervention**: Definitive bleeding control


## Landmark Trials and Evidence

### CRASH-2 Trial (2010)

- **Population**: >20,000 trauma patients with significant hemorrhage
- **Intervention**: TXA 1 g + 1 g infusion vs. placebo
- **Outcome**: **Reduced mortality** (14.5% vs. 16.0%; RR 0.91, p=0.0035)
- **Key finding**: Benefit only if given within 3 hours; harm if delayed >3 hours
- **Clinical implication**: TXA standard of care in bleeding trauma patients [^1_3]


### PROPPR Trial (2015)

- **Population**: 680 severe trauma patients requiring massive transfusion
- **Comparison**: 1:1:1 (PRBC:plasma:platelets) vs. 1:1:2
- **Outcome**: No difference in 24-hour or 30-day mortality
- **Secondary findings**: 1:1:1 achieved hemostasis more effectively; fewer exsanguination deaths
- **Clinical implication**: Supports balanced transfusion; 1:1:1 widely adopted [^1_3]


### PAMPer Trial (2018)

- **Population**: Prehospital trauma transport
- **Intervention**: Prehospital plasma vs. standard care
- **Outcome**: Reduced mortality in patients with prolonged transport (>20 min)
- **Clinical implication**: Prehospital plasma beneficial in select systems


### COMBAT Trial (2018)

- **Population**: Prehospital trauma
- **Intervention**: Prehospital plasma vs. standard care
- **Outcome**: No overall mortality benefit; subgroup analysis suggested benefit in severe shock
- **Clinical implication**: Mixed evidence for prehospital plasma


### TROOP Trial (Whole Blood)

- **Intervention**: Low-titer O-positive whole blood vs. component therapy
- **Outcome**: Improved survival, reduced transfusion requirements
- **Clinical implication**: Whole blood increasingly adopted in trauma centers


## Topic Pearls

★ **TIC affects 25–35% of severely injured trauma patients** and increases mortality 4-fold compared to non-coagulopathic patients with similar injury severity. [^1_3][^1_6]

★ **Pathophysiology triad**: Endothelial activation → Protein C pathway dysregulation → Hyperfibrinolysis with fibrinogen depletion. [^1_1][^1_3][^1_9]

★ **VHA (TEG/ROTEM) is superior to conventional coagulation tests** for rapid detection of hyperfibrinolysis and guiding targeted transfusion therapy. [^1_3]

★ **TXA must be given within 3 hours of injury** (CRASH-2); 1 g IV bolus + 1 g over 8 hours reduces mortality in bleeding trauma patients. [^1_3]

★ **Balanced transfusion (1:1:1 or whole blood)** from resuscitation onset improves hemostasis and reduces exsanguination deaths (PROPPR trial). [^1_3]

★ **Fibrinogen is the first factor to reach critically low levels**; target >150–200 mg/dL with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate. [^1_3]

⚠ **Avoid crystalloids**: They worsen TIC through dilution, acidosis, and endothelial damage; use blood-first resuscitation strategy. [^1_7][^1_3]
