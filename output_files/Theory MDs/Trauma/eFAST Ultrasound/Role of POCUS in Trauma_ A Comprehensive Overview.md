
## Role of POCUS in Trauma: A Comprehensive Overview

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has become an indispensable tool in trauma care, providing rapid, bedside diagnostic information that directly influences resuscitation and management decisions. [^1_1][^1_2] Its primary strength lies in identifying life-threatening, reversible conditions in unstable patients who cannot safely leave the resuscitation area for definitive imaging. [^1_3]

## Core Applications: The eFAST Examination

The **extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (eFAST)** is the cornerstone of trauma POCUS, systematically evaluating for:

### Standard eFAST Views and Targets

| View | Anatomical Target | Pathology Detected |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Pericardial (subxiphoid/parasternal)** | Pericardial space | Pericardial effusion/tamponade |
| **Right Upper Quadrant (Morison's pouch)** | Hepatorenal recess | Intraperitoneal free fluid |
| **Left Upper Quadrant (splenorenal)** | Splenorenal recess | Intraperitoneal free fluid |
| **Pelvic (suprapubic)** | Pouch of Douglas/rectovesical | Pelvic free fluid, bladder injury |
| **Bilateral anterior chest** | Pleural space | Pneumothorax, hemothorax |
| **Bilateral posterior chest** | Costophrenic angles | Hemothorax |

[^1_4][^1_5][^1_6]

### Diagnostic Performance

- **Sensitivity for hemoperitoneum**: ~85–95% in blunt abdominal trauma [^1_7][^1_6]
- **Specificity**: >95% for free fluid detection [^1_7]
- **Pneumothorax detection**: Superior to supine chest radiograph; sensitivity 86–98%, specificity 97–100% [^1_8][^1_6]
- **Time to completion**: <2 minutes by trained operators [^1_5]

**Key limitation**: A negative FAST does **not** exclude intra-abdominal injury, particularly with solid organ injury without significant hemoperitoneum or retroperitoneal bleeding. [^1_9]

## Hemodynamic Assessment in Trauma

POCUS extends beyond eFAST to evaluate shock etiology and guide resuscitation:

### Cardiac POCUS (Focused Cardiac Ultrasound)

- **RV dilation**: Suggests massive pulmonary embolism
- **LV function**: Differentiates cardiogenic from hypovolemic shock
- **Pericardial effusion**: Identifies tamponade physiology
- **IVC assessment**: Collapsibility >50% suggests hypovolemia; plethoric IVC suggests obstructive/distributive shock [^1_8][^1_3]


### Vascular POCUS

- **Aortic assessment**: Detects traumatic aortic injury (limited sensitivity but high specificity)
- **Penetrating neck trauma**: Identifies vascular injury, paratracheal hematoma, tracheal injury [^1_8]
- **DVT screening**: In immobilized trauma patients [^1_8]


## Thoracic Trauma Applications

### Pneumothorax Detection

- **M-mode**: Absence of lung sliding, "barcode sign"
- **B-mode**: Lung point identification (pathognomonic)
- Superior to CXR in supine trauma patients [^1_8][^1_6]


### Hemothorax Detection

- Posterior costophrenic angle views detect fluid earlier than supine CXR
- Quantification possible but operator-dependent [^1_6]


### Pulmonary Contusion

- Subpleural consolidations, B-lines in injured lung zones [^1_1]


## Airway and Procedural Guidance

### Airway POCUS

- **Pre-intubation**: Identifies paratracheal hematoma, tracheal deviation, cricothyroid membrane location
- **Post-intubation**: Confirms endotracheal tube placement (lung sliding, diaphragmatic excursion)
- **Cricothyrotomy guidance**: Landmark identification in difficult airways [^1_8][^1_2]


### Vascular Access

- **Peripheral IV access**: Improves first-attempt success in difficult IV access (DIVA) patients; reduces time to access [^1_10]
- **Central line placement**: Real-time guidance for IJ, subclavian, femoral access
- **Arterial line placement**: Particularly in hypotensive patients [^1_10]


### Other Procedures

- **Thoracostomy**: Identifies optimal site, confirms tube placement
- **Pericardiocentesis**: Guides needle trajectory
- **Nerve blocks**: For analgesia in rib fractures, extremity trauma [^1_8][^1_3]


## Neurological and Head Trauma

### Transcranial Doppler (TCD)

- **ICP estimation**: Elevated pulsatility index suggests increased intracranial pressure
- **Vasospasm detection**: In traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage
- **Cerebral perfusion monitoring**: During resuscitation [^1_8][^1_2]


### Ocular POCUS

- **Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD)**: >5–5.8 mm correlates with elevated ICP
- **Retrobulbar hematoma**: In orbital trauma
- **Globe rupture**: Contraindicates POCUS if suspected [^1_8][^1_2]


## Musculoskeletal Trauma

- **Fracture detection**: Rib, long bone, facial fractures (high specificity, moderate sensitivity)
- **Soft tissue injury**: Hematoma, foreign body localization
- **Compartment syndrome**: Increased muscle compartment echogenicity, loss of fascial planes [^1_1]


## Pre-Hospital and Transport POCUS

### Evidence Base

- Feasible in aeromedical and ground transport settings
- **Rule-in, not rule-out**: Positive findings are actionable; negative scans should not justify down-triage [^1_9]
- **Decision-support tool**: Confirms time-critical problems (tamponade, tension PTX, massive hemothorax) to coordinate with receiving teams [^1_9]


### Limitations in Transport

- Motion artifact, suboptimal positioning, time constraints
- Requires strict indication selection and operator competence [^1_9]


## POCUS in Undifferentiated Shock (Trauma Context)

POCUS rapidly differentiates shock etiology when mechanism is unclear:


| Shock Type | POCUS Findings |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Hypovolemic** | Small hyperdynamic LV, collapsible IVC, empty RV |
| **Cardiogenic** | Depressed LV/RV function, plethoric IVC, B-lines |
| **Obstructive (tamponade)** | Pericardial effusion, RV collapse, plethoric IVC |
| **Obstructive (massive PE)** | RV dilation, McConnell's sign, septal flattening |
| **Distributive** | Hyperdynamic LV, collapsible IVC (early), normal cardiac filling |

[^1_3][^1_6]

## Training and Competency

### Requirements for Proficiency

- **Structured curriculum**: Didactic + hands-on + supervised scans
- **Minimum numbers**: Varies by society (typically 25–50 supervised eFAST exams)
- **Ongoing quality assurance**: Image review, correlation with CT/OR findings [^1_1][^1_3]


### Common Pitfalls

- **False negatives**: Small-volume bleeding, retroperitoneal injury, solid organ injury without hemoperitoneum
- **False positives**: Pericardial fat pad mimics effusion; ascites mistaken for hemoperitoneum
- **Over-reliance**: POCUS complements, not replaces, CT in stable patients [^1_1][^1_9]


## Integration into Trauma Algorithms

### ATLS and POCUS

- POCUS is now embedded in modern ATLS as an adjunct during primary survey
- **Unstable patient**: Positive eFAST → immediate laparotomy/thoracotomy
- **Stable patient**: eFAST guides need for CT; negative eFAST does not exclude injury [^1_2][^1_5]


### Decision Pathways

**Blunt Abdominal Trauma + Hypotension:**

- eFAST positive → OR
- eFAST negative → Consider other sources (chest, pelvis, long bones); DPL or CT if transient responder [^1_4][^1_5]

**Penetrating Torso Trauma:**

- eFAST for tamponade/hemothorax
- Stable patients still require CT or serial exams [^1_4]


## Limitations and Contraindications

### Diagnostic Limitations

- **Operator dependency**: Sensitivity varies with experience
- **Body habitus**: Obesity, subcutaneous emphysema degrade image quality
- **Injury types poorly detected**: Hollow viscus injury, diaphragmatic rupture, pancreatic/duodenal injury, retroperitoneal bleeding [^1_1][^1_9]


### When NOT to Rely on POCUS

- Stable patients where CT is feasible and safe
- Suspected hollow viscus or diaphragmatic injury
- Retained hemothorax quantification
- Definitive exclusion of intra-abdominal injury [^1_9]


## Evidence Summary: Key Trials and Guidelines

| Study/Guideline | Population | Key Finding |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Brenner et al. (multiple)** | Blunt trauma | eFAST reduces time to diagnosis, decreases DPL use |
| **Stengel et al. meta-analysis** | Blunt/penetrating | Sensitivity 79%, specificity 98% for FAST in hemoperitoneum |
| **ACEP Policy Statement** | Emergency POCUS | Endorses eFAST as standard of care in trauma resuscitation |
| **EAST Guidelines** | Blunt abdominal trauma | FAST recommended in hemodynamically unstable patients |
| **WCC/World Federation of Ultrasound** | Global trauma | POCUS improves outcomes in resource-limited settings |

[^1_1][^1_7][^1_3][^1_6]

## Special Populations

### Pediatric Trauma

- Similar eFAST protocol with smaller probes
- Higher sensitivity for hemoperitoneum than adults
- Radiation avoidance makes POCUS particularly valuable [^1_2]


### Pregnant Trauma Patients

- eFAST safe and effective
- Left lateral decubitus positioning for IVC assessment
- Fetal assessment possible but not primary trauma POCUS goal [^1_2]


### Geriatric Trauma

- Anticoagulation increases risk of delayed bleeding
- Serial eFAST may be warranted despite initial negative scan [^1_9]


## Future Directions

### Technological Advances

- **Handheld devices**: Smartphone-connected probes increase accessibility
- **AI-assisted interpretation**: Automated detection of pneumothorax, free fluid
- **Tele-ultrasound**: Remote expert guidance for rural/prehospital settings [^1_1][^1_9]


### Expanding Applications

- **Traumatic aortic injury**: Enhanced TEE/epiaortic POCUS protocols
- **Brain death confirmation**: TCD as adjunct in resource-limited settings
- **Damage control resuscitation**: Serial POCUS for fluid responsiveness, transfusion guidance [^1_8][^1_3]

***

## Topic Pearls

- **eFAST is the trauma POCUS cornerstone**: Pericardial, RUQ, LUQ, pelvic, and bilateral chest views detect tamponade, hemoperitoneum, pneumothorax, and hemothorax in <2 minutes. [^1_4][^1_5]
- **POCUS is a rule-in tool in unstable patients**: Positive findings mandate action; negative scans do not exclude injury and should not delay CT in stable patients. [^1_9]
- **Pneumothorax detection**: POCUS is superior to supine CXR; look for absent lung sliding and lung point. [^1_8][^1_6]
- **Hemodynamic assessment**: Cardiac POCUS differentiates hypovolemic, cardiogenic, and obstructive shock when mechanism is unclear. [^1_3]
- **Procedural guidance**: POCUS improves IV access success, reduces complications, and guides thoracostomy, pericardiocentesis, and nerve blocks. [^1_10]
- **Training matters**: Sensitivity is operator-dependent; structured curricula and ongoing QA are essential. [^1_1]
- **Limitations**: Poor sensitivity for hollow viscus, retroperitoneal, and diaphragmatic injuries; never use POCUS alone to clear the abdomen. [^1_9]
