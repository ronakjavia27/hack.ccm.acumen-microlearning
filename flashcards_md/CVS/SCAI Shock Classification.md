# SCAI Shock Classification

## SCAI Shock Stages (A–E)

| Stage | Title | Clinical Presentation | Hemodynamics | Biomarkers / Perfusion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stage A** | **At Risk** | Acute MI, acute decompensated heart failure; normal exam | Normal SBP and HR | Normal lactate, normal end-organ function |
| **Stage B** | **Beginning** | Tachycardia, hypotension, warm extremities | SBP < 90 mmHg or MAP < 60 mmHg, HR > 100 | Normal lactate, no organ hypoperfusion |
| **Stage C** | **Classic** | Cold, clammy, decreased urine output; needs pressors/MCS | SBP < 90 mmHg, Cardiac Index < 2.2 | Elevated lactate (> 2.0 mmol/L), acute renal/liver injury |
| **Stage D** | **Deteriorating** | Worsening shock despite initial intervention | Escalating pressor requirement or additional MCS | Rising lactate, progressive multiorgan failure |
| **Stage E** | **Extremis** | Cardiac arrest, ongoing CPR, refractory collapse | Severe hypotension despite multiple pressors/MCS | Severe lactic acidosis (often > 10.0 mmol/L) |

## Key Takeaways

**Core concept:** SCAI shock stages grade the severity of cardiogenic shock on a continuum — from at risk (A) to refractory arrest (E) — using clinical exam, hemodynamics, and perfusion markers.

**Key parameters:** Stages are defined by SBP/MAP, HR, cardiac index, and lactate. Stage C is the first stage with clear end-organ hypoperfusion (lactate > 2.0).

**Clinical application:** Use the stage to guide escalation — inotropes/MCS for C, reassess and escalate for D, full resuscitation for E. Reclassify dynamically as the patient improves or deteriorates.

**Interventions:** Early recognition (Stage A/B) prevents progression; moving to MCS at Stage C before multi-organ failure (Stage D) improves outcomes.

**Pitfalls:** Do not anchor on the initial stage — reassess after each intervention; hypoperfusion can exist with normal lactate early in the course.
